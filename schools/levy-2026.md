# The 2026 Levy (Issue 4) and the Five-Year Forecast

[← Back to the CH-UH schools guide](./)

*Cleveland Heights-University Heights City School District (CH-UH), Ohio. State IRN 043794. Researched October 12, 2026.*

This page sets out what is on the ballot, what the district says about its finances, the history of past levies, and the main arguments on each side. OpenHeights does not take a position. Every figure links to its source and gives the date or year it applies to. Where we could not check a figure against a primary document, we say so. See [Gaps / unverified](#gaps--unverified).

> **Verification status: preliminary.** This page was compiled from search-indexed excerpts of the cited sources; during research the primary documents (state report card files, district PDFs, county tables) could not be opened directly. Every figure links to its source and gives its year, but has not yet been checked line-by-line against the original document. Items we could not confirm are listed under "Gaps / unverified." See [About this guide](./#about-this-guide). Starred (\*) figures come from news reports rather than district documents; the five-year forecast PDF itself was not opened.

## Key facts

- **Ballot:** Issue 4, November 3, 2026, in the CH-UH school district ([Cuyahoga County Board of Elections issues list](https://boe.cuyahogacounty.gov/docs/default-source/boe/elections/2026-11-03-issues-list.pdf); [Cleveland 19, Oct. 7, 2026][2]; [Hoodline, Sept. 2026][3]). Majority approval is required.
- **Type:** an **additional** (new) levy for current operating expenses. It is not a renewal or replacement. It is **continuing**, meaning it has no end date and stays until voters change it ([district levy-facts page, via search summary, 2026][1]; [Cleveland 19][2]).
- **Rate:** 6.5 mills ([district levy-facts page][1]; [Cleveland 19][2]).
- **Annual revenue:** **$11,100,193** per year. This is the county's estimate as stated in the ballot language ([district levy-facts page][1]). News reports round it to about $11.1 million.
- **Estimated cost to a homeowner:** **$228 per year for every $100,000** of County Fiscal Officer market value, or about $19 a month ([district levy-facts page][1]; [Cleveland 19][2]). Our own check gives the same answer: 6.5 × 0.35 × $100,000 ÷ 1,000 = **$227.50**.
- **When it would be collected:** If it passes, it applies from tax year 2026. The first payments would be due in calendar year 2027 ([Hoodline][3]).
- **Board votes:** The board adopted the resolution of necessity at a July 21, 2026 special meeting. On August 3, 2026 it voted **unanimously** to send the levy to the ballot ([district news page][4]; [Hoodline][3]).
- **District's stated reason:** The August 2026 five-year forecast projects the June 30 General Fund balance falling from about **$32.2 million in FY2027 to about −$3.7 million in FY2030** ([Hoodline][3]).\*
- **Cuts already made:** The district plans to cut **$5 million** from the General Fund by June 30, 2027, and says **$3.4 million** of that is already in place. On May 6, 2026 the board eliminated **32 positions**. Of those, **21 employees** were laid off (contracts suspended); the others moved into other jobs in the district ([district board post, 2026][5]; [WKYC, 2026][6]; [Hoodline][3]).
- **Levy history:** This is the district's first request for new operating money since November 2020 ([Cleveland 19][2]).

## 1. What is on the ballot

### Ballot language and type

The ballot asks for an additional tax levy for current expenses. It is expected to raise **$11,100,193 a year** at a rate **not to exceed 6.5 mills**, for a **continuing period** ([district levy-facts page, 2026][1]; [search summary of Hoodline/Cleveland 19 reporting][2]).

- **"Additional"** means this is new money on top of the district's existing levies. It does not renew or replace an expiring one.
- **"Continuing"** means the levy has no end date. Voters can still reduce or repeal it through a later ballot measure ([Cleveland 19][2]).

The Board of Elections ballot PDF that appeared in search results ([BOE document][7]) did not load for us. We have **not** checked the full certified wording against it.

### How much it would cost

Ohio taxes homes on 35% of their market value. One mill is $1 of tax for every $1,000 of that taxed value ([Cuyahoga County Fiscal Officer/Auditor explainer][8]; [Heights Observer voters guide][9]).

| Home market value (County Fiscal Officer) | Our calculation (6.5 mills × 35%) | District/county stated figure |
|---|---|---|
| $100,000 | 6.5 × 0.35 × 100,000 ÷ 1,000 = **$227.50/yr** | **$228/yr** ([district][1]; [Cleveland 19][2]) |
| $150,000 | **$341.25/yr** (our calculation) | — |
| $200,000 | **$455.00/yr** (our calculation) | — |
| $300,000 | **$682.50/yr** (our calculation) | — |

The district and county figure matches the standard formula. Use the market value on the County Fiscal Officer's record for your property, not a recent sale price.

**Implied tax base (our calculation):** $11,100,193 ÷ 0.0065 ≈ **$1.708 billion** of taxable (35%) value in the district. This is our own arithmetic from the stated revenue and millage. It is not a figure the district published.

### What the district says the money is for

According to district officials as reported by Cleveland 19, the money would pay for day-to-day operations: teacher pay, busing, utilities, classroom teaching, transportation and special education ([Cleveland 19, Oct. 7, 2026][2]). Operating levies cannot be spent on building projects. Those are paid for separately by bond issues such as the 2013 Issue 81 ([district Bond Issue 81 page][10]).

### What happens if it fails

- The district says its forecast shows the General Fund going into deficit by FY2030 without new revenue ([Hoodline][3]).\*
- In May 2026, Superintendent Liz Kirby told the board the district "could run out of cash by the 2028 to 2029 academic year." She said the cuts would "get us by for the next two years" ([Hoodline, May 2026][11]; [district board post][5]).
- **We did not find a published list of cuts the district would make if Issue 4 fails.** Ohio districts cannot legally end a fiscal year with a negative balance. A failed levy usually leads to more cuts or another ballot attempt. After the 2020 levy failed, for example, the district cut 24 positions, 8 of them teachers ([Cleveland Jewish News, 2020][12]). That is history, not a forecast for 2026.

## 2. The five-year forecast

### What the forecast is

Every Ohio school district must file a five-year General Fund forecast with the state under Ohio Revised Code 5705.391. It is due in November and updated in May. Districts can file extra updates at other times. Each forecast shows three years of actual results, the current year and four years ahead ([Ohio five-year forecast portal][15]).

### Which version we found

| Vintage | What we could verify | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **November 2025** | Not found. | — |
| **May 2026** | Not found directly. In May 2026 the superintendent described cash running out in **school year 2028–29 (FY2029)**. | [Hoodline, May 2026][11] |
| **August 2026** (cited as the basis for the levy) | General Fund balance at June 30 falls from **$32.2M (FY2027)** to **−$3.7M (FY2030)**. The district says it **began deficit spending in FY2025**. It also says the **overall cash balance turns negative by FY2029**. | [Hoodline, Sept. 2026][3] |
| **July 2025** | The board approved a five-year forecast. A board member pointed to "much uncertainty" from changes in federal funding. | [Heights Observer / LWV board highlights, 2025][13] |

**Note on the deficit date.** Reports give two different years, FY2029 and FY2030. There are three possible explanations:

1. The reports describe different measures, such as "cash balance" and "General Fund balance."
2. The August update changed the May numbers.
3. A reporter made an error.

We could not open the forecast PDF to find out which is right. Readers should treat the deficit year as **FY2029–FY2030, depending on the measure and the version**.

### Revenue, spending and assumptions

**We could not get the line-by-line forecast.** That means the figures for property tax, state aid, salaries, benefits and purchased services, along with the treasurer's written assumptions. What the reporting does show:

- **State funding.** The district says the last state budget did not fully phase in Ohio's Fair School Funding Plan. As a result, it expects about **$7 million less in state funding over the next two school years** than it had planned for ([district board post, 2026][5]; [Hoodline][3]). A Heights Observer column describes a **$700,000 decrease** in state aid last year and a **$200,000 decrease** this year compared with 2025. The district had projected a **$7 million increase** ([Heights Observer][14]).
- **EdChoice.** The district has long blamed EdChoice voucher losses for budget pressure. Reported figures: **$4.2M deducted in 2019** and **$6.8M estimated for the following year** ([Patch][16]). A separate analysis put the FY2019 transfer at **$7.36M for 1,132 voucher students** ([NEPC][17]). Since the Fair School Funding Plan (HB 110, 2021), the state has paid for EdChoice directly instead of deducting it from district aid ([NEPC / search summary][17]). So pre-2021 voucher figures do not directly apply to the current forecast. We did not find a 2025 or 2026 figure for any voucher-related effect.
- **HB 920 and property tax growth.** These rules hold voted operating levy revenue roughly flat even when home values rise. See section 4.
- **Salaries and benefits.** We did not find the forecast's assumed raises or health-insurance growth rates. Salaries and benefits are usually the largest part of an Ohio district's spending, but we have no CH-UH-specific figure to cite.

### How sensitive is the deficit date?

Without the full forecast we cannot run the numbers for each assumption. Two simple checks we can do:

- **Our calculation:** A balance falling from +$32.2M (FY2027) to −$3.7M (FY2030) is a drop of **$35.9M over three years**, or about **$12.0M a year** of spending above revenue on average. Issue 4's **$11.1M a year** is about the same size as that average gap. This suggests the levy is sized to roughly close the gap, not to build a large reserve. This is our inference, not a district statement.
- **Reports disagree by a year** (FY2029 vs FY2030). That alone shows the "zero date" moves with which measure and which forecast version is used. Changes in state aid, like the $7M difference above, are about the size of one year's deficit. So the state budget is probably the biggest single factor in when the money runs out.

## 4. Tax burden and why voted levies don't grow with home values

### HB 920 (the "reduction factor")

Ohio's 1976 HB 920 sets "tax reduction factors." When existing property is reappraised and values go up, the effective tax rate on voted levies goes down, so each levy collects about the same dollar amount it did when voters approved it ([Josephine Moore, "Property Taxes in Cleveland Heights: A Primer," 2021][27]; [Miamisburg City Schools HB 920 explainer][28]). New construction does add revenue. A rise in value on existing homes does not.

- **Effect on districts:** This is why Ohio districts that rely on voted levies "must keep returning to voters." When home values rise, revenue from these levies stays flat while costs grow ([Patch, 2020][29]).
- **The 20-mill floor:** Reduction factors cannot push a district's effective rate on current-expense levies below 20 mills. Districts at that floor *do* collect more as values rise. As of tax year 2024, 371 of Ohio's 611 districts were at the floor ([CCAO][30]; [OSBA, Feb. 2026][31]). The 2025–26 law HB 186 limits revenue growth for floor districts to inflation ([LSC analysis][32]). **We did not confirm whether CH-UH is at the 20-mill floor.** Given the district's many voted operating levies, it probably is not, but this is unverified. If it is not, HB 186's floor limit would not directly affect it.

### How CH-UH compares

- The County Auditor says Cuyahoga has the **highest average effective property tax rate in Ohio, 2.44%**. Rates range from about **1.66%** (Cuyahoga Heights) to **3.84%** (Cleveland/Shaker Heights school district overlap) ([Cuyahoga County Auditor][8]). That 3.84% figure is for the Shaker area, **not** CH-UH.
- In tax year 2025, the county's 31 school districts had an average residential rate of about **40.4 mills**, with a median of **39.44 mills** (Solon). This comes from Strongsville's treasurer as reported by Citizen Portal ([Citizen Portal][33]).
- Third-party estimates (methods not public): Ownwell puts Cleveland Heights' median effective rate at **3.79%**, compared with 1.80% for Ohio ([Ownwell][34]). These figures cover all taxing bodies, not just the schools.
- The City of Cleveland Heights lists a total effective real-estate rate of **$117.68 per $1,000 of assessed value** (after rollbacks), covering all taxing bodies, not just the schools ([City of Cleveland Heights, "At a Glance"](https://www.clevelandheights.gov/596/Cleveland-Heights-at-a-Glance)). *OpenHeights calculation:* on a $100,000 home, assessed at $35,000, that is about 35 × $117.68 ≈ **$4,119 a year** in total property tax, or about 4.1% of market value. The source does not give a year.
- The county publishes **Tax Rates by Community (TY2024/CY2025)** ([County Fiscal Officer PDF][35]). We could not extract CH-UH's exact voted and effective residential mills from it. Not yet verified.

**Bottom line:** Every source we found puts the Heights among the higher-taxed communities in Ohio's highest-taxed county. We could not confirm an exact rank or CH-UH's current effective school millage.

## 5. Arguments

We found no named 2026 campaign committee and no organized 2026 opposition group in our searches. In 2020 the organized opposition was the PAC **Tiger Nation 4 Lower Taxes** ([CJN, 2020][12]; [Ideastream][23]). We could not confirm whether it is active in 2026. The arguments below are the strongest evidence-based cases from past and current reporting.

### What supporters argue

- **The deficit is documented.** The district's state-required forecast shows the balance turning negative by FY2029–30 ([Hoodline][3]). Ohio law does not allow districts to run deficits, so the alternative to new revenue is further cuts.
- **Cuts have already been made.** The district has made $3.4M of a $5M reduction plan and eliminated 32 positions (21 layoffs) before asking voters for money ([district][5]; [WKYC][6]).
- **The causes are mostly state decisions.** The Fair School Funding Plan has not been fully phased in, about $7M in expected state aid did not arrive ([district][5]), and EdChoice drew millions from the district in past years ([Patch][16]).
- **Flat revenue is built into Ohio law.** Because of HB 920, earlier levies do not grow with inflation. Districts above the floor must return to voters to keep up with rising costs ([Moore primer][27]).
- **The ask is infrequent and sized to the gap.** It is the first new operating request in six years ([Cleveland 19][2]). Its size roughly matches the projected yearly shortfall (our calculation above).

### What skeptics argue

- **The tax burden is already very high.** Cuyahoga has Ohio's highest effective rates ([Auditor][8]), and the Heights are near the top of the county ([Ownwell][34]). In 2020 opponents argued more levies would make the district among the most heavily taxed in Ohio and would discourage families from moving in ([Ideastream][23]; [Patch][29]). We could not confirm the "most taxed in Ohio" claim.
- **Spending, not funding.** A Heights Observer opinion piece argued the district has "a spending, not funding, problem" ([Heights Observer][36]). In 2020, opponents asked for an independent performance audit before any new levy ([Heights Observer][37]; [Heights Observer][38]). Critics have also said the district's academic results do not justify its costs ([Patch][29]).
- **A continuing levy has no end date.** Voters would need a later ballot measure to remove it ([Cleveland 19][2]).
- **Fixed-income residents are hit hardest.** Older homeowners on fixed incomes carry rising bills ([Patch][29]). New levies passed after 2013 do not get Ohio's 10% and 2.5% rollbacks. That is general Ohio law, which we did not re-verify for this page.
- **The deficit year is uncertain.** Reports disagree by a year (FY2029 vs FY2030), and state funding could change. Skeptics can argue the district could wait or cut more first.
