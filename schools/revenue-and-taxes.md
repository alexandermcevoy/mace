# Revenue, State Aid, and the Property Tax Base

*Cleveland Heights-University Heights City School District (CH-UH), IRN 043687. Research current as of October 12, 2026.*

This page covers where CH-UH's money comes from: local property taxes, state aid under Ohio's Fair School Funding Plan, federal funds, and the deductions that once sent district money to vouchers. It also explains why a home's value can go up sharply while the district's tax revenue does not. The levy itself (Issue 4), spending, enrollment, and EdChoice in depth are on other OpenHeights pages.

> **A note on how this page was built.** Our page-fetching tool could not reach the source websites during this research, so the figures below come from search-engine excerpts of the cited documents. We did not open and read the full PDFs. Each number links to the document it came from. Treat everything here as **provisional until checked against the original**. The "Gaps / unverified" section lists what still needs checking.

## Key facts

- **The district raises most of its money locally.** U.S. News lists annual revenue of **$145.6 million**, with **69.5% local, 15.0% state, and 15.5% federal** [[4]](#sources). It doesn't say which year that covers. NCES reports $145.7 million in total revenue for **FY2022–23** [[5]](#sources). For comparison, Ohio public schools as a whole got about **50.7% local, 36.9% state, and 12.5% federal** in 2023–24 [[6]](#sources).
- **Estimates of the state's share don't agree.** The district's funding page says CH-UH gets **19%** of its budget from the state (the page is undated) [[1]](#sources). The board president's February 2025 testimony put the district's *formula* state share at **13.1%**, which Legislative Service Commission simulations of the Governor's budget projected would fall to **10%** [[2]](#sources). That testimony also said core foundation aid comes to about **$2,300 per student, or "less than $12 million per year"** [[2]](#sources).
- **Voted versus collected.** The district says it has **139.2 mills voted** but collects only **71.2 effective mills** because of HB 920 (the page is undated) [[1]](#sources).
- **The 2024 reappraisal raised values sharply.** Under the county's proposed sexennial results (July 2024), Cleveland Heights home values rose about **44%** on average, compared with **32%** countywide [[7]](#sources). Under HB 920, higher values on their own do not raise what the district collects on its voted levies [[1]](#sources).
- **Budget cuts are under way.** The district announced at least **$5 million in cuts over two years**. It blamed incomplete funding of the Fair School Funding Plan in the state budget, lower state aid caused by rising property values, and recovery from EdChoice losses. The district says full funding of the plan would have brought in about **$7 million more** over two years [[8]](#sources). Reporting says 32 positions were eliminated and 21 employees had their contracts suspended [[9]](#sources). By September 2026, $3.4 million of the cuts had been made [[10]](#sources).
- **Issue 4** is a 6.5-mill additional continuing levy. The district estimates it would raise **$11,100,193 a year**, or **$228 a year per $100,000** of county market value [[11]](#sources).
- **Vouchers used to be deducted from district aid.** The district said EdChoice deductions cost it **$4.2 million** in FY2019, and it projected **$6.8 million** for FY2020 [[12]](#sources). Starting in FY2022, HB 110 has the state pay EdChoice, community (charter) schools, and open enrollment **directly**, so these are no longer deducted from district aid [[13]](#sources).
- **No property-tax abolition vote this year.** The proposed constitutional amendment to abolish Ohio property taxes did **not** make the November 2026 ballot. Organizers say they will try for 2027 [[14]](#sources).

**How to read this.** The different "state share" numbers measure different things.

- The **13.1%** figure is the Fair School Funding Plan's *state share percentage*: the share of the formula's base cost the state pays after it calculates how much the district can raise locally.
- The **15%** and **19%** figures are shares of *total revenue*. These may include state money outside the formula, such as reimbursements for the 10%/2.5% property-tax rollbacks and the homestead exemption. That is an OpenHeights hypothesis we have not verified against the district's books.
- The **federal 15.5%** figure probably reflects pandemic-era ESSER relief money, which has since expired. That is also unverified, because the year is unclear.

**OpenHeights calculation (rough).** Foundation aid of "less than $12 million" divided by about $145.6 million in total revenue comes to about **8%** ($12M ÷ $145.6M ≈ 0.082). The years and fund definitions don't match, so this only gives the order of magnitude. It suggests the formula aid itself is a small part of the budget.

## 2. The Fair School Funding Plan as applied to CH-UH

**How the formula works.** The Fair School Funding Plan (HB 110, 2021) sets a per-pupil **base cost**. It then estimates a district's **local capacity** from a blend of property value per pupil and residents' income, and the state pays the rest (the **state share**) [[18]](#sources). Statewide, the base cost per pupil went from **$6,020 in FY2021 to $8,240 in FY2025**, according to the plan's advocates [[18]](#sources). The plan was designed to phase in over **six years, or three state budgets** [[19]](#sources).

**Guarantees.** Districts whose formula amount comes out *below* what they received before can get "temporary transitional aid." For FY2024–FY2025, this aid kept each district from falling below its FY2020 funding base. It equals the FY2020 base minus core foundation aid [[20]](#sources). Rising property values, falling enrollment, and counting income in the capacity measure can all push a district onto the guarantee [[21]](#sources).

**Is CH-UH a "guarantee" or "phase-in" district?** We don't know yet. The district's statements point to formula pressure from rising property values and a falling state share: "rising property values, resulting in a decrease in state aid" [[8]](#sources), and the projected drop from 13.1% to 10% [[2]](#sources). Those conditions often lead to a guarantee, but we could not confirm CH-UH's status or the dollar amount of any guarantee from its FY2026 School Finance Payment Report. See Gaps.

**What the FY2026–27 state budget (HB 96, 2025) changed.**
- **Before passage:** The Governor proposed reaching 83% of the phase-in in FY26 and 100% in FY27, with cost inputs frozen at older data. The House took the phase-in out of its version [[22]](#sources). Policy Matters Ohio says the final budget appropriated **$2.75 billion less** for FY26–27 than the plan called for [[23]](#sources).
- **CH-UH's testimony** in May 2025 cited a 3.1-point cut in its state share percentage and a **17.1% cut to special education additional aid** (9.4% in FY26 and 8.5% in FY27). The district said it would have to make these up through local levies [[24]](#sources). These figures describe versions of the bill still under debate. We have not confirmed the final numbers for CH-UH.
- **What the district says now:** at least $5 million in cuts, and about $7 million that full implementation would have provided over two years [[8]](#sources).
- **A conflicting account:** One fall 2025 district forecast (for a different school system) said HB 96 "continues the last two years of the phase in," with FY2027 as the final phase-in year [[22]](#sources). Advocates instead say the final phase was abandoned [[23]](#sources). Different groups describe the outcome differently.

**OpenHeights calculations:**
- *Implied reduction:* 1 − (71.2 ÷ 139.2) = **0.489**. About 49% of CH-UH's voted millage is cancelled by HB 920 reduction factors across all classes combined. The district did not say whether its 71.2 figure is the residential or the commercial effective rate.
- *Issue 4 cost check:* $100,000 market value × 35% assessment = $35,000 assessed value. $35,000 × 0.0065 = **$227.50**, which matches the district's $228.
- *Implied tax base:* $11,100,193 ÷ 6.5 mills = $1,707,722 per mill. Since one mill raises one dollar per $1,000 of assessed value, that implies a taxable assessed value of roughly **$1.71 billion**. This is an approximation from the levy estimate, not an official valuation. The county's certified valuation may differ.

**Mostly residential base.** The district says it relies on property taxes alone for local revenue (there is no school income tax) [[1]](#sources). We could not get the official split between residential/agricultural (Class I) and commercial/industrial (Class II) value this session, and the figures for tax abatements, TIF districts, and delinquency are also missing. See Gaps.

**County ranking.** Third-party estimates of Cuyahoga County's median effective tax rate run from 1.8% to 2.3% of market value, and these are among the highest in Ohio [[26]](#sources). We did not find a reliable district-by-district ranking that places CH-UH. The Cuyahoga County Treasurer's "Tax Rates by Community" page lists voted and effective rates by community [[27]](#sources).

## 4. How HB 920 and "reduction factors" work

1. **A mill is $1 per $1,000 of assessed value.** In Ohio, assessed value is **35% of market value**. One mill on a $100,000 home costs $35 a year [[1]](#sources).
2. **Voted levies are frozen in dollars.** Under HB 920 (1976), when the county revalues property (a full reappraisal every six years and an update every third year), it applies a **reduction factor** to each voted levy so the levy raises the same total dollars as before. New construction is the exception [[1]](#sources). The voted millage stays on paper, but the **effective millage** falls.
3. **That's how CH-UH has 139.2 voted mills but collects 71.2** [[1]](#sources). Inflation and higher home values do not increase the district's levy revenue. To get more money, the district has to ask voters for new millage. This is why the district returns to the ballot periodically.
4. **Residential and commercial property are tracked separately.** Class I (residential/agricultural) and Class II (commercial/industrial) values get their own reduction factors and effective rates. When home values rise faster than commercial values, as in 2024, the residential effective rate falls more.
5. **The 20-mill floor.** Reduction factors can't push a district's effective rate for current operating expenses below **20 mills**. Districts at the floor collect more as values rise. HB 186 (signed in 2025) caps that growth for floor districts at an inflation measure (the three-year GDP deflator), with state money to hold districts harmless for losses during this reappraisal period [[28]](#sources). CH-UH's effective rate is far above 20 mills, so the cap on floor districts **probably does not affect CH-UH directly** (OpenHeights inference).
6. **Inside (unvoted) millage.** The 10 mills Ohio allows without a vote are *not* subject to reduction factors, so the district's share of inside millage grows as values rise. HB 335 limits that growth in reappraisal and update years to the three-year GDP deflator, and it adjusts the school funding formula to partly make up the difference [[28]](#sources). We don't know how much inside millage CH-UH has.
7. **Why reappraisal can cut state aid.** The state formula counts property value per pupil as part of local capacity. When values rise 44% but voted-levy revenue stays flat, the formula assumes the district can raise more locally, and the state share can fall. The district gives this as one reason for its cuts [[8]](#sources).

Issue 4 is a new *additional* levy, so it is not directly affected. |

## 6. Money that passes through: EdChoice, community schools, open enrollment

- **Before FY2022**, Ohio paid for EdChoice vouchers by **deducting** them from the resident district's state aid. CH-UH reported deductions of **$4.2 million (FY2019)** and a projected **$6.8 million (FY2020)** [[12]](#sources).
- The district has also said that in FY2022 it received only about **$1,700 per pupil** in core foundation funding, while about **$11 million** went to roughly 2,000 EdChoice students living within its boundaries [[32]](#sources). That figure is a district claim about money flowing to students in the area. It is not a deduction from CH-UH's own budget.
- **Since FY2022**, HB 110 has the state fund EdChoice, community (charter) schools, and open enrollment **directly** instead of deducting them from districts [[13]](#sources). The district still cites "recovery from EdChoice voucher losses" as a source of financial strain [[8]](#sources). CH-UH was a plaintiff in the EdChoice lawsuit, in which a Franklin County judge ruled the program unconstitutional in June 2025. The program continues while the case is appealed [[33]](#sources). The EdChoice page has more detail.

- **Year of the U.S. News revenue split** is not stated. The 15.5% federal share may reflect ESSER.
- **Tax base details:** Total assessed valuation by class, residential versus commercial share, Class I and II effective rates, University Heights' reappraisal change, abatements/TIFs, delinquency, inside millage, valuation per pupil compared with the state median, and CH-UH's county or state millage rank were all **not found**. Sources: Ohio Department of Taxation SD-1 tables, Cuyahoga County Fiscal Officer abstracts, and the County Treasurer's rate table [[27]](#sources).
- **"32.2% vs 43.3%" state share:** An advocacy piece cites this figure, but it is unclear whether it means CH-UH or the whole state. We did not use it.
- **Contradictions found:**
  - **Phase II funding:** Reaching Heights (2023) said Phase II was "fully funded" [[19]](#sources). The district's later testimony and statements describe cuts and incomplete implementation [[8]](#sources), [[24]](#sources). These may refer to different budgets.
  - **What HB 96 did:** Accounts differ on whether HB 96 *continued* the phase-in [[22]](#sources) or *abandoned* the final phase [[23]](#sources).
  - **State share:** 19% (district), 15% (U.S. News), and 13.1% (formula) are different measures, not necessarily errors.
