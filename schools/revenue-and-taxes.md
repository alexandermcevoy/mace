# Revenue, State Aid, and the Property Tax Base

[← Back to the CH-UH schools guide](./)

*Cleveland Heights-University Heights City School District (CH-UH), IRN 043794. Research current as of October 12, 2026.*

This page covers where CH-UH's money comes from: local property taxes, state aid under Ohio's Fair School Funding Plan, federal funds, and the deductions that once sent district money to vouchers. It also explains why a home's value can go up sharply while the district's tax revenue does not. The levy itself (Issue 4), spending, enrollment, and EdChoice in depth are on other OpenHeights pages.

> **Verification status: checked against primary sources on Oct. 12, 2026**, except items listed under "Gaps / unverified." Revenue shares, peer comparisons, valuation, and millage come from the Ohio Department of Education and Workforce (DEW) District Profile ("Cupp") Reports for FY2020–FY2025. State-aid figures come from DEW's School Finance Payment Report (SFPR) data files for FY2025–FY2027. Ten-year revenue comes from the district's audited Annual Comprehensive Financial Reports (ACFRs), and community tax rates from the Cuyahoga County Treasurer. Several earlier figures were outdated and have been corrected; see the [Corrections log](#corrections-log). See [About this guide](./#about-this-guide).

## Key facts

- **The district raises most of its money locally.** In FY2025, CH-UH's revenue was **65.7% local taxes, 7.5% other local non-tax revenue, 18.3% state, and 8.5% federal**, or **$26,942 per pupil** in total. Statewide averages were 46.8% local, 7.9% other, 37.0% state, and 8.3% federal, or $18,352 per pupil [[34]](#sources). NCES reports **$145.7 million** in total revenue for FY2022–23: 71% local, 16% state, and 13% federal [[5]](#sources).
- **The three "state share" numbers measure different things.** The **18–19%** figure is the state's share of *all* revenue, and about **7.9 points** of it are state reimbursements of homeowners' property-tax rollbacks and homestead credits. The **13.1%** figure (FY2025) is the formula's *state share percentage*. It fell to the legal **minimum of 10%** in FY2026 and FY2027 [[34]](#sources), [[36]](#sources). See section 1.
- **State formula aid is falling.** Core foundation funding was **$10.17 million in FY2025, $8.88 million in FY2026** (−12.7%), and **$8.82 million in FY2027** (October 2026 payment). Total net state funding stayed about flat, at **$13.26M → $12.93M → $13.30M**, because transportation and other aid rose [[36]](#sources). CH-UH is **not** on a funding guarantee.
- **Voted versus collected.** For current operating expenses, CH-UH has **149.5 voted mills**. After the 2024 reappraisal it collected an effective **51.05 mills on homes (Class I)** and **84.63 mills on business property (Class II)** in tax year 2024 [[34]](#sources). The district's often-quoted "139.2 voted / 71.2 effective" dates from about tax year 2014 [[15]](#sources).
- **The 2024 reappraisal raised values sharply.** CH-UH's taxable value rose from **$1.256 billion (TY2023) to $1.704 billion (TY2024), +35.6%**. Residential/agricultural value rose about **41%** and other real property about **10%** (OpenHeights calculation from DEW data) [[36]](#sources), [[34]](#sources). Countywide, the proposed home-value increase was 32% [[7]](#sources).
- **Very high tax rates, above-average wealth.** CH-UH's property value per pupil was **$364,394** (TY2024), about 36% above the median Ohio district. Its Class I effective operating millage was the **4th-highest of 606 districts** [[34]](#sources). For tax year 2025, the total residential tax rate (all governments combined) was about **2.86% of market value in Cleveland Heights and 2.88% in University Heights**. That is among the five highest of the 81 rate areas in Cuyahoga County [[40]](#sources).
- **Budget cuts are under way.** The district announced at least **$5 million in cuts over two years**. It blamed incomplete funding of the Fair School Funding Plan, lower state aid caused by rising property values, and recovery from EdChoice losses [[8]](#sources). The SFPR data bear out the property-value part of that explanation (section 2).
- **Issue 4** is a 6.5-mill additional continuing levy. The district estimates it would raise **$11,100,193 a year**, or **$228 a year per $100,000** of market value [[11]](#sources). The revenue estimate is exactly 6.5 mills × CH-UH's **TY2025 taxable value of $1,707,722,020** [[36]](#sources).
- **No property-tax abolition vote this year.** The proposed constitutional amendment did **not** make the November 2026 ballot [[14]](#sources).

## 2. The Fair School Funding Plan as applied to CH-UH

**How the formula works.** The plan (HB 110, 2021) sets a per-pupil **base cost** for each district. It then estimates the district's **local capacity** per pupil from a blend of property value and residents' income. The state pays the difference, but never less than **10%** of the base cost [[37]](#sources).

**Guarantee or phase-in?** CH-UH is a **phase-in district, not a guarantee district**. Its calculated formula amount has stayed above its FY2020 funding base of $7.21 million, so it received a growing share of the increase. No temporary transitional aid was paid in any of the three years [[36]](#sources).

**Why state aid fell.** Local capacity per pupil rose from $7,103 to $8,495 (+19.6%) as the 2024 reappraisal entered the capacity measure. Over the same period the base cost was essentially flat ($8,177 → $8,181). The state share percentage therefore fell to the 10% floor, and core foundation funding fell **$1.29 million (−12.7%)** from FY2025 to FY2026, even with the phase-in rising from 66.67% to 83.33% (OpenHeights calculation from [[36]](#sources)). This matches the district's statement that rising property values reduced state aid [[8]](#sources). In February 2025 the board president's testimony projected that the share would fall from 13.1% to 10%; that projection was correct [[2]](#sources).

**What HB 96 (FY2026–27 budget) did, reconciled.** DEW says HB 96 "continued the phase-in" at 83.33% in FY2026 and 100% in FY2027 [[37]](#sources). In CH-UH's files, the statewide average base cost per pupil ($8,241.61) and statewide average teacher salary ($68,022.22) are **identical in FY2025, FY2026, and FY2027** [[36]](#sources). In other words, the phase-in finished, but the cost inputs were not updated. Policy Matters Ohio calls the budget **$2.75 billion** short of the full plan [[23]](#sources). The two accounts are therefore not contradictory: one is about the phase-in percentage and the other about outdated inputs (OpenHeights reconciliation).

**District testimony (May 2025)** cited a 3.1-point cut in the state share percentage, which matches the final 13.1% → 10.0%, and a 17.1% cut to special education aid [[24]](#sources). In the final SFPR, CH-UH's special education funding was **$1,037,056 (FY2025) and $920,164 (FY2026), −11.3%**. Its calculated, pre-phase-in amount fell from $1,303,523 to $1,003,409 [[36]](#sources). The testimony's 17.1% referred to a bill version still under debate.

**OpenHeights calculations:**
- *Reappraisal effect on homeowners' effective rate:* Class I effective operating millage fell from 70.02 to 51.05 mills (−27%) between TY2023 and TY2024, while residential value rose about 41%. This is HB 920 working as designed: voted levies collect about the same dollars as before.

## 4. How HB 920 and "reduction factors" work

1. **A mill is $1 per $1,000 of assessed value.** In Ohio, assessed value is **35% of market value**. One mill on a $100,000 home costs $35 a year [[1]](#sources).
2. **Voted levies are frozen in dollars.** Under HB 920 (1976), when the county revalues property (a full reappraisal every six years and an update every third year), it applies a **reduction factor** to each voted levy so the levy raises the same total dollars as before. New construction is the exception [[1]](#sources).
3. **For CH-UH this means 149.5 voted operating mills but 51.05 effective residential mills in TY2024** [[34]](#sources). Higher values do not raise voted-levy revenue; new revenue requires new millage.
4. **Residential and commercial property are tracked separately.** Class I and Class II get separate reduction factors. In 2024 home values rose far faster than commercial values (about +41% versus about +10% for CH-UH), so the residential effective rate fell much more (70.0 → 51.1) than the commercial rate (90.8 → 84.6) [[34]](#sources), [[16]](#sources).
5. **The 20-mill floor.** Reduction factors can't push a district's effective current-expense rate below **20 mills**. HB 186 (2025) limits revenue growth for districts at the floor [[28]](#sources). CH-UH is far above the floor, so **HB 186 probably does not affect it directly** (OpenHeights inference).
6. **Inside (unvoted) millage** is not reduced by HB 920. CH-UH had **4.45 inside mills** in TY2020 [[35]](#sources). HB 335 (effective March 18, 2026) limits growth from inside millage in reappraisal and update years [[28]](#sources).
7. **Why reappraisal can cut state aid.** The formula's local capacity measure uses property value. When values rise 36% but voted-levy revenue stays flat, the formula assumes the district can raise more locally. CH-UH's state share then falls, as happened in FY2026 (section 2).

## 6. Money that passes through: EdChoice, community schools, open enrollment

- **Before FY2022**, Ohio paid for EdChoice vouchers by **deducting** them from the resident district's state aid. CH-UH reported deductions of **$4.2 million** for the prior fiscal year (reported as 2019) and projected **$6.8 million** for the following year [[12]](#sources).
- The district has said that in FY2022 it received only about **$1,700 per pupil** in core foundation funding, while about **$11 million** went to roughly 2,000 EdChoice students living within its boundaries [[32]](#sources). This is a district claim about money flowing to students in the area. It is not a deduction from CH-UH's budget.
- **Since FY2022**, HB 110 has the state fund EdChoice, community schools, and open enrollment **directly** [[13]](#sources), [[37]](#sources). This change largely explains the drop in CH-UH's state revenue share between FY2021 and FY2022 (section 1). CH-UH's SFPR shows no open-enrollment adjustment [[36]](#sources). CH-UH was a plaintiff in the EdChoice lawsuit; a Franklin County judge ruled the program unconstitutional in June 2025, and it continues during appeal [[33]](#sources).
