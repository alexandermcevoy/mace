# EdChoice, Private Schools, and School Choice in the Heights

[← Back to the CH-UH schools guide](./)

*Last researched: October 12, 2026. This page is part of OpenHeights' nonpartisan coverage of the Cleveland Heights-University Heights City School District (CH-UH, IRN 043794) ahead of the November 3, 2026 vote on Issue 4, a 6.5-mill continuing operating levy. It explains private-school vouchers and other choice programs and what they mean for CH-UH. It takes no position on the levy or on vouchers. Every figure has a source and a year. Where the sources disagree, we say so.*

## Key facts

- **Vouchers used to be paid out of the district's state aid. Now the state pays them directly.** Until FY2021, Ohio subtracted EdChoice scholarships from a student's home district's state aid (the "deduction" model). The FY2022–23 state budget, [HB 110 (2021)](https://www.ralaw.com/media/insights/Public%20Law%20Alert/new_funding_mandates_for_k-12_schools_in_ohio_s_operating_budget), switched to having the state pay scholarships directly [[1]](#sources).
- **In 2019–20, before the switch, CH-UH reported a $7,074,113 EdChoice deduction.** A school board member gave this figure in the [Heights Observer (Oct. 2020)](https://heightsobserver.org/read/2020/10/23/the-7074113-edchoice-deduction-was-real-money-lost) and projected $9,017,250 for 2020–21 [[2]](#sources). A rebuttal letter accepted the $7.07M but said the state had credited the district **$4,286,412** for those same voucher students. That would leave a net cost of about $2.8M [[3]](#sources).
- **About 1,400 CH-UH residents used EdChoice in 2019–20, and most had never attended the district.** A News 5 Cleveland analysis of 2019–20 found that **1,326 of 1,406** CH-UH voucher users had never enrolled in a CH-UH school (94.3%, OpenHeights calculation: 1,326 ÷ 1,406) [[4]](#sources), [[5]](#sources).
- **We found no reliable count for after 2020.** Coverage cites "1,800 students / $11 million" from court filings [[6]](#sources) and "$11 million / 2,000 students" [[7]](#sources). We could not confirm the year behind either figure, and neither comes from state data (see *Gaps*).
- **The program has grown a lot statewide since every family became eligible.** In 2024–25, EdChoice Expansion (income-based) paid **about $493 million** for **100,939 students** ([DEW 2025 Scholarship Annual Report](https://education.ohio.gov/getattachment/About/Annual-Reports/2025-Scholarship-Annual-Report.pdf.aspx?lang=en-US)) [[8]](#sources). Of the **~25,700** first-time Expansion recipients in FY2025, **~6,100 (24%)** had been in a public school the year before ([LSC, Dec. 2025](https://www.lsc.ohio.gov/assets/organizations/legislative-service-commission/files/edchoice-expansion-scholarships-december-2025.pdf)) [[9]](#sources).
- **CH-UH is a named plaintiff in the constitutional lawsuit against EdChoice.** The suit was filed January 4, 2022. Franklin County Judge Jaiza Page ruled on **June 24, 2025** that EdChoice is unconstitutional, then stayed her ruling. The 10th District Court of Appeals heard oral argument on **May 12, 2026**. **We found no appellate decision as of this research** [[10]](#sources)–[[13]](#sources).

## How EdChoice works

Ohio runs several private-school scholarship ("voucher") programs. This page covers the two EdChoice programs.

| Program | Who is eligible | Amount (2025–26 max) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Traditional (performance-based) EdChoice** | Students assigned to public school buildings the state designates as low-performing on report-card measures | $6,166 (K–8) / $8,408 (9–12) [[14]](#sources) | Eligibility depends on the building, not family income. About 42,607 students statewide "this fiscal year," as reported in May 2026 [[11]](#sources) |
| **EdChoice Expansion (income-based)** | Since HB 33 (2023), every Ohio K–12 student outside the Cleveland Metropolitan district. Families at or below 450% of the federal poverty level get the full amount; the amount shrinks as income rises above that [[9]](#sources), [[15]](#sources) | Same maximums, prorated by income above 450% FPL | 100,939 students and about $493M in 2024–25 [[8]](#sources). In FY2024: 86,850 students and $404.6M [[16]](#sources) |

Cleveland residents use a separate Cleveland Scholarship. CH-UH is its own district, so its residents use EdChoice [[16]](#sources).

**How the amounts have changed.** Under the deduction model in 2019–20, a voucher took **$4,650 (K–8)** or **$6,000 (high school)** out of the home district's state aid ([Ideastream, Feb. 2020](https://www.ideastream.org/education/2020-02-17/edchoice-has-cleveland-heights-university-heights-schools-in-limbo)) [[4]](#sources). HB 110 (2021) raised the maximums to **$5,500 / $7,500** [[1]](#sources). By 2023–24 they were **$6,166 / $8,408** [[17]](#sources), and they remain there for 2025–26 [[14]](#sources).

## What "cost" to CH-UH means: before and after 2021

**Before FY2022 (deduction model).** The state counted voucher students who lived in a district as part of that district's enrollment. It credited the district some state aid for them, then subtracted the full scholarship from the district's state payment. The two sides of the local fight in 2020 read the same numbers differently:

| Item (2019–20) | Amount | Source |
|---|---|---|
| EdChoice deduction from CH-UH state aid | $7,074,113 | Board member op-ed [[2]](#sources) |
| State funding credited for those voucher students (per rebuttal) | $4,286,412 | Letter to the editor [[3]](#sources) |
| Net effect if the credit is counted | $2,787,701 | OpenHeights calculation: $7,074,113 − $4,286,412 |
| Projected 2020–21 deduction | $9,017,250 | Board member op-ed [[2]](#sources) |

The board member said the credit was not real money. CH-UH's state funding was frozen on a "cap," so it did not grow with student counts, while the deductions did [[2]](#sources). The rebuttal said the credit has to be netted out [[3]](#sources). Both views have a basis. Under a cap, extra students on paper may bring no extra dollars, so the deduction comes out of money the district would otherwise have had. Leaving the credit out entirely, though, can overstate the loss. OpenHeights cannot settle this from the sources available. The district's audited FY2020 state-aid statements would.

Other figures from the same period, in different years and on different bases:

- **FY2019:** CH-UH sent about **$7.36 million** in state funding to nonpublic schools for **1,132** voucher students. This comes from an NEPC analysis as summarized by the Ohio Coalition for Equity & Adequacy of School Funding, an advocacy group and a plaintiff in the lawsuit [[18]](#sources).
- **CH-UH's own statement (date not confirmed, likely 2019):** about **1,300** students, "most of whom have never attended our public schools," with "an actual loss of $4.2 million … last fiscal year and an estimated loss of $6.8 million this fiscal year" ([CH-UH news article](https://www.chuh.org/protected/ArticleView.aspx?iid=6GA3GPB&dasi=3I02)) [[19]](#sources).
- **2019–20:** The superintendent said the district had to transfer **$7 million** to private schools that year [[5]](#sources). An opinion column put the combined loss to vouchers *and charter schools* at **over $12 million** that year [[20]](#sources).

**From FY2022 on (direct funding).** HB 110 ended the deductions. The state now pays scholarships and charter schools directly [[1]](#sources). So when someone says vouchers "cost CH-UH $X million" for a year after FY2021, that is not a line-item subtraction from CH-UH's state aid. What remains is indirect:

1. **Competition for state money.** Vouchers and public-school aid both come out of the state budget. Critics compare per-student amounts. One analysis found CH-UH students received about **$2,600 per pupil** in state funding in 2023–24, compared with $6,166 or $8,408 per voucher [[17]](#sources). See also [WYSU (Aug. 2023)](https://wysu.org/ohio-news/2023-08-17/private-school-vouchers-now-higher-than-state-funding-many-ohio-public-schools-get-per-student) [[21]](#sources). CH-UH gets relatively little state aid per pupil because the state formula considers it able to raise more locally.
2. **Enrollment.** When a student who would otherwise attend CH-UH takes a voucher, the district loses the per-pupil state aid for that student. It may also save some variable costs. Statewide, about three-quarters of new Expansion recipients in FY2025 were not coming from public schools [[9]](#sources).
3. **Transportation.** Ohio law requires districts to bus resident K–8 students to private and charter schools, with fines for failing to do so (see below) [[22]](#sources).

## Why voucher use in CH-UH has been high

- **Building designations.** Under the traditional program, families qualify through their assigned building. The state's 2019–20 designated list included CH-UH's **Noble** and **Oxford** elementary schools [[23]](#sources). The state was set to nearly triple the number of eligible buildings for 2020–21, and **nine** Heights schools were on the expanded list. The legislature then delayed and changed that expansion [[4]](#sources), [[24]](#sources), [[25]](#sources).
- **Many private schools nearby.** Cleveland Heights is home to several Orthodox Jewish schools. The [Cleveland Jewish News (2025)](https://www.clevelandjewishnews.com/features/special_sections/education/edchoice-scholarships-ruled-unconstitutional-jewish-day-schools-affected/article_e8d82c0e-982c-42c5-a115-5d3d25ced10c.html) lists the **Hebrew Academy of Cleveland** and **Yeshiva Derech Hatorah** (both in Cleveland Heights), **Fuchs Mizrachi** and **Mandel Jewish Day School** (Beachwood), and **Gross Schechter** (Pepper Pike) among the schools affected by the ruling [[26]](#sources). An older report said **685 of the Hebrew Academy's 1,250** students used vouchers. That is 54.8% (OpenHeights calculation: 685 ÷ 1,250), but the year is not confirmed [[26]](#sources). The area also has Catholic and other religious schools, including Gesu, Communion of Saints (formerly St. Ann), Beaumont, Ruffing Montessori, and Lutheran East. *None of the sources we retrieved document their voucher counts or which CH-UH residents attend them.*
- **Most users were already in private school.** Across eight Northeast Ohio districts in 2019–20, **4,013 of 6,319** voucher users (63.5%) had never enrolled in the district whose aid paid for them. CH-UH's share was higher, at 1,326 of 1,406 [[5]](#sources). The district's CFO later gave a similar figure of about 94% [[4]](#sources).

## The constitutional lawsuit (*Columbus City Schools et al. v. State of Ohio*)

| Date | Event | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Jan. 4, 2022 | The Vouchers Hurt Ohio coalition files suit in Franklin County Common Pleas Court. Named plaintiffs: Columbus, **CH-UH**, Richmond Heights, Lima and Barberton districts, **two Cleveland Heights parents**, and the Ohio Coalition for Equity & Adequacy of School Funding. About 100 districts were coalition members at filing. | [[10]](#sources), [[27]](#sources) |
| June 24, 2025 | Judge Jaiza Page grants the plaintiffs summary judgment on **3 of 5 counts**. She holds that EdChoice creates an unconstitutional parallel system of private schools, in violation of Article VI, Section 2, and **stays** her ruling, so scholarships continue. | [[12]](#sources), [[28]](#sources), [[29]](#sources) |
| July 2025 | Attorney General Dave Yost appeals to the 10th District Court of Appeals. | [[30]](#sources) |
| Jan. 2026 | Both sides file reply briefs. | [[27]](#sources) |
| Feb. 2026 | A bill is introduced to withhold state funding from districts that sue the state. School districts criticize it. (Its status is not verified.) | [[31]](#sources) |
| May 12, 2026 | Oral argument before a three-judge 10th District panel, reported to be all Democrats. | [[11]](#sources), [[13]](#sources) |
| As of Oct. 12, 2026 | **We found no reported appellate decision.** Coverage expects the losing side to take the case to the Ohio Supreme Court. | [[11]](#sources), [[32]](#sources) |

The **plaintiffs** argue that the Constitution's "thorough and efficient system of common schools" clause bars funding a second, private system, and that vouchers deepen segregation and drain public money [[10]](#sources). The **state** argues that the scholarships go to families, not schools, and are lawful [[11]](#sources). Reported coalition size has grown from about 100 districts in 2022 to "around 200" in 2025 and "more than 325" in a 2026 podcast description [[27]](#sources). Those are coalition figures, not independently verified.

## Other choice programs that affect CH-UH families

| Program | What it is | Status / figures |
|---|---|---|
| **Scholarship donation tax credit** | Ohio taxpayers get a credit for gifts to certified scholarship-granting organizations: up to **$750 per person**, or $1,500 for a married couple filing jointly when each spouse gives $750. The credit is limited to the donor's tax liability. | [[33]](#sources) |
| **ACE (Afterschool Child Enrichment)** | Accounts for tutoring, camps and enrichment. Created in 2021 with **$125M** in federal COVID relief money. Started at $500 per child and rose to **$1,000** in 2023. | Payments paused in 2025 after federal extensions were withdrawn. A third-party listing says the program closed **Oct. 15, 2025** [[34]](#sources), [[35]](#sources) |
| **Community (charter) schools** | Publicly funded, independently run schools, paid directly by the state since FY2022 [[1]](#sources). | We found **no current count** of CH-UH residents in charter schools (see *Gaps*). |
| **Inter-district open enrollment** | Each district decides whether to accept non-resident students. About one in six Ohio districts, mostly around big cities, do not [[36]](#sources). | We **could not confirm** CH-UH's current policy. |
| **Transportation to private/charter schools** | Districts must bus resident K–8 students to nonpublic and charter schools and face penalties if they don't. AP (Sept. 2025) reported some districts cutting public-student routes as voucher-related demand grew. HB 545, the "School Busing Improvement Act," was introduced in Oct. 2025. | [[22]](#sources), [[37]](#sources). We found no CH-UH-specific cost figures. |

## What the research says

**Test scores.** The [Fordham Institute's 2016 study by David Figlio and Krzysztof Karbownik](https://fordhaminstitute.org/ohio/research/evaluation-ohios-edchoice-scholarship-program-selection-competition-and-performance) used student-level data. It found that students who used vouchers scored *worse* on state tests than closely matched peers who stayed in public schools, with larger losses in math. It also found voucher users were relatively less disadvantaged than eligible non-users [[38]](#sources), [[39]](#sources). Fordham supports school choice and called the results "disappointing" [[39]](#sources).

**Competitive effects.** The same study found that EdChoice modestly *improved* the scores of eligible students who stayed in public schools, which the authors attributed to competition [[40]](#sources). Critics such as Diane Ravitch say the design cannot show that competition caused the improvement [[40]](#sources).

**College outcomes.** An [Urban Institute study by Chingos, Figlio and Karbownik](https://www.urban.org/research/publication/effects-ohios-edchoice-voucher-program-college-enrollment-and-graduation) followed EdChoice students who started between 2007–08 and 2013–14. **64%** enrolled in college, compared with **48%** of matched public-school peers, and **23%** earned a bachelor's degree, compared with **15%**. The effects were largest for students who stayed in the program four or more years [[41]](#sources). The authors note that earlier studies found negative test-score effects [[41]](#sources).

**Who uses vouchers now.** Since universal eligibility, coverage has reported vouchers [going increasingly to higher-income families and students already in private school (2023)](https://www.ideastream.org/education/2023-06-05/school-vouchers-now-going-to-more-wealthy-private-school-students) [[42]](#sources). LSC's 24% figure for prior public-school attendance is consistent with that [[9]](#sources).

**District finances.** Fordham has published an analysis by Ohio State's Stéphane Lavertu on how EdChoice affects district enrollment, finances and academics [[43]](#sources). We were not able to retrieve its findings. The national group EdChoice (which has no connection to the Ohio program) estimates that choice programs saved taxpayers $19.4–45.6 billion nationally through FY2022. It also argues that districts' true savings when a student leaves equal only their short-run variable costs [[44]](#sources).

## What voucher supporters argue

- Scholarships go to **families**, not institutions. Parents, including those of faith, should be able to choose a school that fits their child without paying twice, through taxes and tuition [[11]](#sources).
- Long-run outcomes such as college enrollment and graduation matter more than short-term test scores, and Ohio's evidence there is positive [[41]](#sources).
- Competition modestly improved scores for eligible students who stayed in public schools [[40]](#sources).
- Since FY2022, vouchers are no longer subtracted from district aid [[1]](#sources). When a child who was never in the district receives a scholarship, the district loses nothing it was ever paid for that child.
- Groups representing Jewish and Catholic schools, such as Agudath Israel, say ending EdChoice would hurt families who depend on it [[28]](#sources).

## What the district and critics argue

- A second, state-funded private system is unconstitutional, and it grows while public per-pupil aid lags. CH-UH gets about $2,600 per pupil from the state, compared with up to $8,408 per voucher [[17]](#sources).
- Most local voucher users never attended CH-UH (94% in 2019–20) [[5]](#sources). Critics say this means the program mainly subsidizes tuition families were already paying, rather than rescuing students from struggling schools.
- Under the old deduction model, CH-UH lost several million dollars a year from its state aid ($7.07M gross in 2019–20) [[2]](#sources). That shaped the district's finances and levy history.
- The test-score evidence on voucher users is negative [[38]](#sources). Private schools also do not take the same state tests or follow the same accountability rules.
- Transportation mandates and growing statewide voucher spending still put pressure on districts after direct funding [[22]](#sources).

## How this relates to Issue 4

Since FY2022, EdChoice scholarships for CH-UH residents are not subtracted from CH-UH's budget. Claims that "vouchers cost CH-UH $X per year" describe either the pre-2021 deductions or indirect effects. Voters weighing Issue 4 should ask which of the two a given figure means, and what year it comes from.
