# EdChoice, Private Schools, and School Choice in the Heights

[← Back to the CH-UH schools guide](./) · [See this in charts](charts.html#families)

> **Verification status: checked against primary sources on Oct. 12, 2026**, except items listed under *Gaps / unverified*. The district-level counts and dollar figures below come from the Ohio Department of Education & Workforce's (DEW) final School Finance Payment Reports (SFPRs) and EdChoice designated-building list. Statewide figures come from DEW and the Legislative Service Commission. Opinion pieces and advocacy sources are labeled as such. **Main limit:** since FY2022 the state's public payment reports no longer list how many CH-UH residents use vouchers or charter schools. *Last researched: October 12, 2026. This page is part of OpenHeights' nonpartisan coverage of the Cleveland Heights-University Heights City School District (CH-UH, IRN 043794) ahead of the November 3, 2026 vote on Issue 4, a 6.5-mill continuing operating levy. It explains private-school vouchers and other choice programs and what they mean for CH-UH. It takes no position on the levy or on vouchers. Every figure has a source and a year. Where the sources disagree, we say so.*

## Key facts

- **Vouchers used to be paid out of the district's state aid. Now the state pays them directly.** Until FY2021, Ohio subtracted scholarships and charter-school funding from the home district's state aid (the "deduction" model). The FY2022–23 state budget, [HB 110 (2021)](https://www.ralaw.com/media/insights/Public%20Law%20Alert/new_funding_mandates_for_k-12_schools_in_ohio_s_operating_budget), switched to direct state payments [[1]](#sources). DEW's payment reports show the change. CH-UH's FY2021 report has an EdChoice line; its FY2022–FY2026 reports have no scholarship or charter-school deduction lines [[45]](#sources).
- **Before the switch, CH-UH had the highest EdChoice share of resident students in Ohio.** The state counts every resident student it funds, including those using vouchers or attending charters. In FY2021, EdChoice students were **24.4%** of all CH-UH resident students the state counted (1,776.55 of 7,273.89 full-time equivalents). That was **#1 of 609 districts**. CH-UH also ranked #1 in FY2019 (13.4%) and FY2020 (20.0%). Second place was Jefferson Township Local (17.1%) in FY2021 and Euclid City in FY2019. (OpenHeights calculation from DEW's final SFPRs [[45]](#sources); see the ranking section below.)
- **The state deducted $7.08 million for EdChoice from CH-UH's aid in FY2020 and $8.94 million in FY2021** (DEW final SFPRs) [[45]](#sources). The FY2020 figure is close to the **$7,074,113** a school board member cited in 2020 [[2]](#sources). For FY2020, a rebuttal letter cited a **$4,286,412** state credit for the same students. We could reproduce that credit almost exactly from state data (see below) [[3]](#sources).
- **About 1,400 CH-UH residents used EdChoice in 2019–20, and most had never attended the district.** DEW counts 1,404.72 EdChoice FTEs for FY2020 [[45]](#sources). A News 5 Cleveland analysis found that **1,326 of 1,406** CH-UH voucher users had never enrolled in a CH-UH school (94.3%) [[4]](#sources), [[5]](#sources).
- **The "about 1,800 students / more than $11 million" figure is for FY2022.** The plaintiffs' side (an OEA magazine feature reprinted by the Ohio Coalition for Equity & Adequacy of School Funding) gives it as FY2022 [[18]](#sources). It is not state data. DEW's last published count, for FY2021, is 1,776.55 FTEs [[45]](#sources).
- **Four CH-UH buildings are EdChoice-designated for 2026–27:** Cleveland Heights High School, Baumgardner Middle School (the former Monticello), Roxboro Middle School and Oxford Elementary. Statewide, **513 of 2,877** listed buildings are designated, spread across **78** districts ([DEW 2026–27 designated list](https://education.ohio.gov/getattachment/Topics/Other-Resources/Scholarships/EdChoice-Scholarship/EdChoice-Resources/Designated-List-2026-2027-With-Criteria.xlsx.aspx?lang=en-US)) [[46]](#sources).
- **The program has grown a lot statewide since every family became eligible.** In 2024–25, EdChoice Expansion (income-based) paid **$492.9 million** for **100,939 students**, and traditional EdChoice paid **$283.1 million** for **42,607** ([DEW 2025 Scholarship Annual Report](https://education.ohio.gov/getattachment/About/Annual-Reports/2025-Scholarship-Annual-Report.pdf.aspx?lang=en-US)) [[8]](#sources). Of the **~25,700** first-time Expansion recipients in FY2025, **~6,100 (24%)** had been in a public school the year before ([LSC, Dec. 2025](https://www.lsc.ohio.gov/assets/organizations/legislative-service-commission/files/edchoice-expansion-scholarships-december-2025.pdf)) [[9]](#sources).
- **CH-UH is a named plaintiff in the constitutional lawsuit against EdChoice, and the appeal is still pending.** In June 2025 a Franklin County judge ruled EdChoice unconstitutional and stayed her ruling. The 10th District Court of Appeals heard argument on **May 12, 2026**. **We found no appellate decision as of Oct. 12, 2026.** A coalition newsletter dated Aug. 21, 2026 still described the case as pending [[53]](#sources).

## How EdChoice works

Ohio runs several private-school scholarship ("voucher") programs. This page covers the two EdChoice programs.

| Program | Who is eligible | Amount (2025–26 max) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Traditional (performance-based) EdChoice** | Students assigned to public school buildings the state designates as low-performing on report-card measures | $6,166 (K–8) / $8,408 (9–12) [[14]](#sources) | Eligibility depends on the building, not family income. 42,607 students and $283.1M in FY2025 [[8]](#sources) |
| **EdChoice Expansion (income-based)** | Since HB 33 (2023), every Ohio K–12 student outside the Cleveland Metropolitan district. Families at or below 450% of the federal poverty level get the full amount; the amount shrinks as income rises above that [[9]](#sources), [[15]](#sources) | Same maximums, prorated by income above 450% FPL | 100,939 students and $492.9M in FY2025 (average $4,958) [[8]](#sources). In FY2024: 86,850 students and $404.6M [[16]](#sources) |

Cleveland residents use a separate Cleveland Scholarship. CH-UH is its own district, so its residents use EdChoice [[16]](#sources). The DEW annual report and news accounts give slightly different FY2025 Expansion counts (100,939 vs. 100,930 to 100,941) [[8]](#sources), [[11]](#sources), [[13]](#sources). We use DEW's figure.

**How the amounts have changed.** Under the deduction model in 2019–20, a voucher took **$4,650 (K–8)** or **$6,000 (high school)** out of the home district's state aid ([Ideastream, Feb. 2020](https://www.ideastream.org/education/2020-02-17/edchoice-has-cleveland-heights-university-heights-schools-in-limbo)) [[4]](#sources). HB 110 (2021) raised the maximums to **$5,500 / $7,500** [[1]](#sources). By 2023–24 they were **$6,166 / $8,408** [[17]](#sources), and they remain there for 2025–26 [[14]](#sources).

The board member argued the credit was not real money, because CH-UH's state aid was capped and did not grow with student counts [[2]](#sources). State data partly support this. In FY2019, CH-UH's formula funding was held by the state's cap: it received $21.31 million against $23.18 million "subject to cap," a cap ratio of 0.888 (FY2019 SFPR) [[45]](#sources). The FY2020 report bases its formula aid on FY2019 figures, so the district's aid did not rise when EdChoice use jumped from 881 to 1,405 FTEs that year [[45]](#sources). The rebuttal's view that the credit must be netted out reflects how the formula credits students on paper [[3]](#sources). Both readings are arithmetically correct. They answer different questions: what the formula credited, versus whether the district would have had that money without vouchers.

Other figures from the same period, checked where possible:

- **FY2019:** An NEPC analysis, as summarized by the Ohio Coalition (an advocacy group and plaintiff), said CH-UH sent about **$7.36 million** to nonpublic schools for **1,132** voucher students [[18]](#sources). DEW's final FY2019 report shows **$7,331,494** in total scholarship deductions. The matching FTE counts are 881.04 EdChoice + 213.53 Jon Peterson + 34.79 autism = **1,129.36** [[45]](#sources). The two sources are consistent.
- **2019–20 combined loss:** An opinion column put the combined loss to vouchers *and charter schools* at **over $12 million** [[20]](#sources). DEW's FY2020 report gives $10,183,493 in scholarship deductions plus $2,634,226 in charter-school deductions, **$12.82 million** in total (OpenHeights calculation) [[45]](#sources).
- **CH-UH's own statement (undated, probably 2019):** about **1,300** students, "an actual loss of $4.2 million … last fiscal year and an estimated loss of $6.8 million this fiscal year" ([CH-UH news article](https://www.chuh.org/protected/ArticleView.aspx?iid=6GA3GPB&dasi=3I02)) [[19]](#sources). We could not match these to a specific year in the SFPRs, so they remain the district's claim.

**From FY2022 on (direct funding).** HB 110 ended the deductions. The state now pays scholarships and charter schools directly [[1]](#sources). So when someone says vouchers "cost CH-UH $X million" for a year after FY2021, that is not a line-item subtraction from CH-UH's state aid. The FY2022 "1,800 students / $11 million" figure, for example, is what the state paid private schools for CH-UH residents, not a deduction from CH-UH [[18]](#sources). What remains is indirect:

1. **Competition for state money.** Vouchers and public-school aid both come out of the state budget. One analysis found CH-UH students received about **$2,600 per pupil** in state funding in 2023–24, compared with $6,166 or $8,408 per voucher [[17]](#sources); see also [WYSU (Aug. 2023)](https://wysu.org/ohio-news/2023-08-17/private-school-vouchers-now-higher-than-state-funding-many-ohio-public-schools-get-per-student) [[21]](#sources). In FY2026, the state's formula treats CH-UH as able to fund most of its base cost locally: its state share of the base cost is **10%** ($817.86 of $8,178.58 per pupil) (FY2026 Final #2 SFPR, Detailed tab) [[45]](#sources).
2. **Enrollment.** When a student who would otherwise attend CH-UH takes a voucher, the district loses the per-pupil state aid for that student. It may also save some variable costs. Statewide, about three-quarters of new Expansion recipients in FY2025 were not coming from public schools [[9]](#sources).
3. **Transportation.** Ohio law requires districts to bus resident K–8 students to private and charter schools, with penalties for failing to do so [[22]](#sources). We found no CH-UH-specific cost figure.

## Why voucher use in CH-UH has been high

- **Building designations.** Under the traditional program, families qualify through their assigned building. A copy of the state's 2019–20 designated list included CH-UH's **Noble** and **Oxford** elementary schools [[23]](#sources). The state was set to nearly triple the number of eligible buildings for 2020–21, and **nine** Heights schools were on the expanded list. The legislature then delayed and changed that expansion [[4]](#sources), [[24]](#sources), [[25]](#sources).
- **2026–27 designations (DEW).** Four CH-UH buildings are designated: Cleveland Heights High School, Baumgardner Middle, Roxboro Middle and Oxford Elementary. Each qualified under "Option B": its Performance Index ranked in the state's bottom 20% in two of the three prior years, and the district's Title I formula percentage averaged 20% or more (CH-UH's average is 20.6%). The other six listed buildings are not designated: Gearity, Boulevard, Canterbury, Fairfax, Noble and Roxboro Elementary [[46]](#sources). Students assigned to designated buildings can get a traditional scholarship regardless of income. All other residents can apply for income-based Expansion scholarships.
- **Many private schools in and near the district.** See the table below. Cleveland Heights is home to several Orthodox Jewish schools. The [Cleveland Jewish News (2025)](https://www.clevelandjewishnews.com/features/special_sections/education/edchoice-scholarships-ruled-unconstitutional-jewish-day-schools-affected/article_e8d82c0e-982c-42c5-a115-5d3d25ced10c.html) lists the Hebrew Academy of Cleveland and Yeshiva Derech Hatorah (Cleveland Heights) and Fuchs Mizrachi, Mandel Jewish Day School and Gross Schechter (outside the district) among schools affected by the ruling [[26]](#sources).
- **Most users were already in private school.** Across eight Northeast Ohio districts in 2019–20, **4,013 of 6,319** voucher users (63.5%) had never enrolled in the district whose aid paid for them. CH-UH's share was higher, at 1,326 of 1,406 [[5]](#sources). The district's CFO later gave a similar figure of about 94% [[4]](#sources).

## What the research says

**Test scores.** The [Fordham Institute's 2016 study by David Figlio and Krzysztof Karbownik](https://fordhaminstitute.org/ohio/research/evaluation-ohios-edchoice-scholarship-program-selection-competition-and-performance) used student-level data. It found that students who used vouchers scored *worse* on state tests than closely matched peers who stayed in public schools, with larger losses in math. It also found voucher users were relatively less disadvantaged than eligible non-users [[38]](#sources), [[39]](#sources). Fordham supports school choice and called the results "disappointing" [[39]](#sources).

**Competitive effects.** The same study found that EdChoice modestly *improved* the scores of eligible students who stayed in public schools, which the authors attributed to competition [[40]](#sources). Critics such as Diane Ravitch say the design cannot show that competition caused the improvement [[40]](#sources).

**College outcomes.** An [Urban Institute study by Chingos, Figlio and Karbownik (April 22, 2025)](https://www.urban.org/research/publication/effects-ohios-edchoice-voucher-program-college-enrollment-and-graduation) found that EdChoice students were more likely to enroll in college than matched public-school peers (**64% vs. 48%**) and to earn a bachelor's degree (**23% vs. 15%**). The effects were largest for the roughly 60% of students who stayed in the program four or more years. The study covers the program before universal eligibility, and the authors say it is too early to judge the current program [[41]](#sources).

**Who uses vouchers now.** Since universal eligibility, coverage has reported vouchers [going increasingly to higher-income families and students already in private school (2023)](https://www.ideastream.org/education/2023-06-05/school-vouchers-now-going-to-more-wealthy-private-school-students) [[42]](#sources). LSC's 24% figure for prior public-school attendance is consistent with that [[9]](#sources).

**District finances: what the main Ohio studies found.**

- **Fordham / Stéphane Lavertu (Ohio State), Dec. 2022** [[43]](#sources), [[50]](#sources). This study is funded by a pro-choice organization. It compares districts with more vs. less exposure to performance-based EdChoice from 2006–07 to 2018–19. For the average exposed district by 2019, it estimates:
  - enrollment **10–15% lower** than it would otherwise have been;
  - **no difference in total spending per pupil**;
  - **10–15% higher local revenue per pupil**, because local taxes stay with the district while enrollment falls;
  - no significant effect on property values, tax rates, levy passage or fiscal-stress designations;
  - higher district Performance Index scores, which the author says partly reflects low-achieving students leaving.

  The study says its design could not produce causal estimates for income-based EdChoice. It covers the years before universal eligibility.
- **Fordham / Vladimir Kogan, "Don't blame EdChoice for district budget woes," July 20, 2026** [[51]](#sources). This is a commentary by a pro-choice author. It argues that Ohio districts' FY2026 state aid came in about **$1 billion above** what they projected in spring 2022, before universal eligibility, and that **86%** of districts received more than they expected. It also argues that declining enrollment, not vouchers, is driving fiscal stress. It notes this "is not definitive proof" that vouchers do not affect enrollment.
- **Policy Matters Ohio (Piet van Lier), Feb. 25, 2020** [[52]](#sources). This group opposes vouchers. Writing under the deduction model, it noted that the performance-based voucher's cost was "deducted from [districts'] state funding and for many districts exceeds the per-pupil amount they receive from the state." It cites research finding no academic advantage for private schools.
- **Ohio Education Policy Institute (Howard Fleeter)** and **Buckeye Institute.** We did not retrieve an OEPI fiscal study. News coverage reports Fleeter's finding that the share of EdChoice students who had attended private school the year before jumped from 7% in 2019 to nearly 55% after 2020 rule changes [[15]](#sources). The Buckeye Institute filed an amicus brief supporting EdChoice in the lawsuit. We found no Buckeye fiscal-impact study.
- **The national EdChoice organization** (no connection to the Ohio program) estimates that choice programs saved taxpayers $19.4–45.6 billion nationally through FY2022 [[44]](#sources). It also argues that districts' true savings when a student leaves equal only their short-run variable costs.

**How these apply to CH-UH.** The Lavertu study measures averages across districts and ends in 2019, before CH-UH's EdChoice share went from 13% to 24%. It does not estimate effects for any single district. In CH-UH, about 94% of 2019–20 voucher users had never enrolled in the district [[5]](#sources). A voucher for a student who would never have enrolled in CH-UH changes neither the district's enrollment nor its costs. Before FY2022, though, the state still subtracted that voucher from CH-UH's aid.

## What voucher supporters argue

- Scholarships go to **families**, not institutions. Parents, including those of faith, should be able to choose a school that fits their child without paying twice, through taxes and tuition [[11]](#sources).
- Long-run outcomes such as college enrollment and graduation matter more than short-term test scores, and Ohio's evidence there is positive [[41]](#sources).
- Competition modestly improved scores for eligible students who stayed in public schools [[40]](#sources). Statewide, Lavertu found no drop in districts' per-pupil spending [[50]](#sources).
- Since FY2022, vouchers are no longer subtracted from district aid [[1]](#sources). When a child who was never in the district receives a scholarship, the district loses nothing it was ever paid for that child. Statewide district aid has exceeded districts' own pre-expansion forecasts [[51]](#sources).
- Groups representing Jewish and Catholic schools, such as Agudath Israel, say ending EdChoice would hurt families who depend on it [[28]](#sources).

## What the district and critics argue

- A second, state-funded private system is unconstitutional, and it grows while public per-pupil aid lags. CH-UH gets about $2,600 per pupil from the state, compared with up to $8,408 per voucher [[17]](#sources).
- Most local voucher users never attended CH-UH (94% in 2019–20) [[5]](#sources). Critics say this means the program mainly subsidizes tuition families were already paying, rather than rescuing students from struggling schools.
- Under the old deduction model, CH-UH had Ohio's highest EdChoice share of resident students. It lost $7.08M (FY2020) and $8.94M (FY2021) in EdChoice deductions, and $12.8M (FY2020) and $15.3M (FY2021) once all scholarship and charter deductions are counted [[45]](#sources). That shaped the district's finances and levy history.
- The test-score evidence on voucher users is negative [[38]](#sources). Private schools also do not take the same state tests or follow the same accountability rules.
- Transportation mandates and growing statewide voucher spending still put pressure on districts after direct funding [[22]](#sources).

## How this relates to Issue 4

Since FY2022, EdChoice scholarships for CH-UH residents are not subtracted from CH-UH's budget. Claims that "vouchers cost CH-UH $X per year" describe either the pre-2022 deductions or indirect effects. Voters weighing Issue 4 should ask which of the two a given figure means, and what year it comes from. The documented deductions for FY2019–FY2021 are in the tables above.
