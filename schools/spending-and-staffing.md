# Spending, Staffing, and Facilities

[← Back to the CH-UH schools guide](./) · [See this in charts](charts.html#spending)

*Cleveland Heights-University Heights City School District (CH-UH). Research current as of October 12, 2026. This page covers how the district spends money and how it is staffed. Separate pages cover the Issue 4 levy and five-year forecast, revenue and taxes, enrollment, academics, and EdChoice.*

> **Verification status: checked against primary sources on Oct. 12, 2026**, except items listed under "Gaps / unverified." Spending, staffing, salary, audit, debt and transportation figures were taken directly from state and federal data files and the district's audited financial report (see Sources). News-reported items about the 2026 cuts, past contracts and the 2013 bond campaign come from the cited news and district pages and were not re-checked line by line. A Corrections log is at the end. See [About this guide](./#about-this-guide).

## Key facts

- **CH-UH spends more per pupil than the state average and most neighbors, on every measure.** On Ohio's report card measure, which weights for student need, CH-UH spent **$18,548 per "equivalent pupil"** on the 2025–26 report card. The state figure was **$13,078**. CH-UH ranked **2nd-highest of 96** districts in its size group; Shaker Heights was 1st [1]. On the federal (Census/NCES) measure, CH-UH spent **$25,025 per enrolled pupil in FY2024**, compared with **$17,257 statewide**, or about **45% more** [2][3].
- **That gap has narrowed slightly over time.** From FY2014 to FY2024, CH-UH's per-pupil current spending rose about **41%**, while the Ohio average rose about **52%** [2][3][4]. (OpenHeights calculation; see Section 1.)
- **The classroom share is low.** On the 2025–26 report card, **63.1%** of CH-UH spending went to classroom instruction, compared with **66.9% statewide** and 68.5% for similar-size districts. CH-UH ranked **92nd of 96** in its size group [1].
- **Staffing vs. enrollment.** By the district's own count of full-time employees, staff rose from **890 in FY2016 to 956 in FY2025 (+7%)**, while enrollment (average daily membership) fell from **5,221 to 4,649 (−11%)**. Instructional staff fell 14%. Pupil-support staff rose 71% [5]. Federal (NCES) data shows teachers falling **21%** (441 to 348 FTE) from 2015–16 to 2024–25 while enrollment fell 6% [6].
- **Two staff totals, both correct.** NCES counts **1,211.91 full-time-equivalent staff** for 2024–25 [6][7]. The district's audited report counts **956 full-time employees** for FY2025 [5]. The 32 positions cut in 2026 are 2.6% of the first figure and 3.3% of the second, so both fit the district's "approximately 3%."
- **Teacher pay.** The state's FY2025 District Profile lists CH-UH's average classroom teacher salary as **$87,540**, against a **$76,482** statewide average [8]. The 2025–26 report card shows **$90,331** and an average of **16 years of experience** [1]. The **2025–2028 teachers' contract** raises the base salary **3.25% a year** and grants a full experience step each year, plus a one-time **$750** stipend. The employee health-premium share rises from 11% to 12% in July 2027 [9].
- **Audits are clean.** The FY2024 and FY2025 financial audits (Rea & Associates, released by the Auditor of State) found **no findings, no material weaknesses, and no reportable noncompliance**. The district qualified as a "low-risk auditee" [10][11].
- **Compared with districts serving similarly poor students, CH-UH is near the top on both classroom and non-classroom spending.** It spends $11,697 per equivalent pupil in classrooms (97th percentile of poverty-similar districts) and $6,851 outside them, 1.8 times the peer median. The low classroom share reflects high non-classroom spending, not thin classrooms.
- **Buildings are under-filled more than empty.** Two buildings (15% of space) are vacant. Operating schools average about 11 students per classroom in grades K–8. Excess space likely costs roughly $2–4.6 million a year.
- **Busing mostly serves nonpublic students.** In the FY2026 state funding report, **1,139 of the 1,710 students** CH-UH carries on school buses (**67%**) attend nonpublic schools [12].

## 1. Total and per-pupil spending

### Why per-pupil figures disagree

| Measure | What it divides | CH-UH | State | Year | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Report-card expenditure per *equivalent* pupil | Operating spending ÷ need-weighted enrollment | $18,548 | $13,078 | 2025–26 report card | [1] |
| DEW District Profile (Cupp) "total operating expenditure per pupil" | Operating spending ÷ enrolled ADM | $27,676 | $17,609 (statewide average of districts) | FY2025 | [8] |
| Census/NCES current spending per pupil | Day-to-day spending ÷ fall enrollment | $25,025 | $17,257 | FY2024 | [2][3] |
| Census/NCES current spending per pupil | same | $24,221 (instruction $13,896, 57%) | $16,476 | FY2023 | [2][3][7] |
| NCES total expenditures per student (includes capital and debt) | $138,037,000 ÷ 5,010 students | $27,552 | — | FY2023 | [7] |
| District ACFR "per pupil cost" | General-fund spending ÷ ADM | $26,102 | — | FY2025 | [5] |

**What "per equivalent pupil" means.** Ohio's report card divides spending by a weighted enrollment. Students with disabilities, economically disadvantaged students and English learners count as more than one pupil. Plain "per pupil" measures count every student once [13]. CH-UH has a large share of higher-need students: **21.9% of students had disabilities in FY2025**, against a 16.9% state average [8]. So it looks less expensive per *equivalent* pupil than per *enrolled* pupil. Even after weighting, it remains about **42% above the state figure** (OpenHeights calculation: $18,548 ÷ $13,078 ≈ 1.42) [1].

The figures CH-UH officials cited in about 2020 match FY2019 state data: **$21,222 per pupil** (District Profile) and **$14,404 per equivalent pupil** (report card) [14][8][1].

### Compared with districts serving similarly poor students

Neighboring districts differ a lot in poverty, so we also compared CH-UH with every Ohio district whose child-poverty rate is close to CH-UH's (18.9%; Census SAIPE 2024). We used Census child poverty because Ohio's "economically disadvantaged" flag is 99.9% for CH-UH, an artifact of universal free meals (see the [enrollment page](enrollment-and-demographics)). *OpenHeights calculation from the 2025–26 report-card spending data for all 607 districts [1] and Census SAIPE [33].*

| Per equivalent pupil, 2025–26 report card | CH-UH | Median of 30 districts with 15–23% child poverty and 2,500+ students | CH-UH percentile in that group |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total spending | $18,548 | $12,103 | 97th |
| Classroom spending | $11,697 | $8,514 | 97th |
| Non-classroom spending | $6,851 | $3,724 | 97th |
| Classroom share | 63.1% | 69.0% | 3rd |

- **The low classroom share does not mean classrooms are short-changed.** CH-UH spends more per pupil *in* classrooms than almost any comparable district. Its share is low because non-classroom spending is about 1.8 times the peer median.
- **The result holds however the peer group is drawn.** Against all 126 districts with 15–23% child poverty, all 186 at CH-UH's poverty level or higher, Ohio's urban districts, or CH-UH's own state typology group, CH-UH ranks between the 93rd and 98th percentile on both classroom and non-classroom dollars.
- **Controlling for child poverty, disability share and size** in a statewide regression, CH-UH spends about **$6,400 more per equivalent pupil than predicted** (98th percentile of residuals).
- **Caveat.** Ohio's need weighting counts economically disadvantaged students as more than one pupil. CH-UH's inflated disadvantage flag therefore probably *understates* its spending relative to peers.

### Spending on resident students who do not attend district schools

Ohio districts must bus many resident students who attend private and charter schools, and they pass state "auxiliary services" money through to nonpublic schools.

- **Busing.** The state's FY2026 foundation payment report counts **1,710 CH-UH school-bus riders: 571 public and 1,139 nonpublic (67%)**. Nonpublic routes accounted for 574 weighted miles, compared with 495 for public routes [12]. The FY2025 report counted 1,298 nonpublic and 619 public riders (68% nonpublic). FY2024 counted 1,190 and 522 (70%) [12]. The state pays only about 10% of the formula transportation amount for CH-UH because of the district's local wealth ("state share" 0.10) [12].
- **Transportation cost.** Audited pupil-transportation expenses were **$4.36 million (FY2023), $4.85 million (FY2024) and $5.40 million (FY2025)** [5][10]. The district does not publish a split between public and nonpublic riders, so we do not estimate the nonpublic share in dollars.
- **Auxiliary services.** In FY2025 the district received $998,301 in state auxiliary-services funds and spent $1,287,079 on services and materials for the ten nonpublic schools in its boundaries [5].
- **EdChoice (history).** In about 2019–20, about 1,400 resident students used EdChoice vouchers. The district reported a cost of roughly $7 million [15][16]. A 2020 Heights Observer letter put the 2019–20 deduction at $7,074,113, with $4,286,412 funded by the state, for a net of about $2.79 million [17]. Federal finance data shows CH-UH paid **$8.79 million to private schools and $3.04 million to charter schools in FY2021**, the last year before direct state funding [4]. EdChoice is covered on its own page.

### Administrators

- **DEW District Profile, FY2025:** 39 administrator FTEs, average salary **$121,031** (state average $101,274), and **120 pupils per administrator** (state average 125; similar districts 103) [8]. In FY2015 the figures were 38 FTEs and $105,744 [8]. By headcount, CH-UH is not administrator-heavy. Its administrators are paid about 20% more than the state average, and administration spending per pupil is about 1.9 times the state average (see above).
- **NCES, 2024–25:** 4.0 district administrators, 23.8 school administrators, and 112.4 administrative-support staff [7].
- **District count:** 51 full-time "administration" employees in FY2025, up from 43 in FY2016 [5]. This function category includes support staff in principals' offices.

CH-UH's average teacher salary rose 19% from FY2015 to FY2025 (OpenHeights calculation). ## 3. Recent cuts (2026)

| Item | Detail | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Board action | May 6, 2026: 32 positions eliminated, effective July 1, 2026 | [19] |
| Positions | 11 classified staff, 7 instructional coaches, 4 Title I teachers (the district cites federal funding changes), 4 administrators, 2 social workers, and others | [19][26] |
| People affected | 21 contracts suspended; others moved into open positions through bumping rights | [19][27] |
| Savings | ~$3.4M in the general fund from reductions in force, plus cuts to department budgets, professional development and supplemental positions | [19][27] |
| District's stated cause | Incomplete phase-in of the state Fair School Funding Plan. The district says full implementation would have added about $7M over two years | [19] |

Per position, the reductions average about **$106,000 in salary and benefits** (OpenHeights calculation: $3.4M ÷ 32 ≈ $106,250). This is a rough average, since some savings may come from non-RIF items.

**Earlier precedent.** In 2016 the district cut more than 50 positions, mostly teachers, to save about $3.5 million per year, citing enrollment decline [18]. NCES data shows teacher FTEs falling from 441 to 393 that year [6].

**Two readings.** Critics of district spending point to several facts. CH-UH has the second-highest need-weighted spending in its size group and a classroom share near the bottom. Its support-staff ratios are far richer than similar districts', and non-teaching staff grew while enrollment fell. The district and union point to cost drivers outside the district's control: a high share of students with disabilities (21.9% vs. 16.9% statewide) [8] and near-universal economic disadvantage [8]. Most busing serves nonpublic students [12], and the district lost voucher deductions before FY2022 [4]. They also note that teacher counts have already fallen faster than enrollment [6]. A Heights Observer column argued the 2026 cuts carry a "heavy cost" for students [28].

**What this shows:**

- **Only two buildings are empty.** Wiley and Millikin hold about 202,500 square feet, 15% of the district's space. Vacant buildings cost relatively little to carry (heat, security, insurance), but the district does not report that cost.
- **The larger issue is under-filled operating schools.** The district averages about 11 students per classroom in its elementary and middle schools and about 238 square feet per student across its operating schools. Classroom counts include rooms for special education, art, music and intervention, so these are not class sizes. But they point to substantial unused capacity, especially in seven small elementary schools averaging about 285 students each.
- **Rough cost of excess space (OpenHeights estimate).** CH-UH's building operation and maintenance cost about $12.7 million in FY2024, or about $11.16 per square foot of non-vacant space [2]. School planning commonly runs roughly 150–200 square feet per student; this range is our assumption, not a verified state standard. Against it, CH-UH's operating schools carry about **177,000–409,000 square feet (16–37%) more space than needed**, costing roughly **$2.0–$4.6 million a year**. That is about a third to three-quarters of the **$6.1 million** gap between CH-UH's maintenance spending and the poverty-peer median ($1,239 × 4,916 pupils). The rest reflects higher cost per square foot, which is plausible for buildings mostly built in the 1920s and 1930s.
- **History.** The district has closed or repurposed several buildings as enrollment fell: Millikin, Wiley (after 2013–14), Coventry (transferred to Heights Libraries) and Taylor (now the Delisle Options Center). The 2012 facilities plan proposed closing three elementary schools; that has not happened. The May 2025 ten-year plan relies on the 3.8-mill permanent-improvement levy (about $3.6 million a year) and proposes no consolidation [34]. Heights High was rebuilt under the 2013 bond at 372,334 square feet for a school that now enrolls about 1,400 students.
- **Debt capacity.** The same presentation reports $129.6 million in debt against a $153.3 million legal limit, leaving $23.7 million of capacity [34].

### Legal costs

Residents often cite legal fees from lawsuits against the district. What the record shows:

- **No comparable statewide figure exists.** Ohio's district finance reports do not break out legal fees, so CH-UH's legal spending cannot be ranked against other districts from published data.
- **Individual bills appear in board agendas, not totals.** Examples from invoice-approval lists:
  - $32,640 to Mazanec Raskin & Ryder (superintendent's office, Aug. 2020);
  - $50,000 to Squire Patton Boggs (charged to Finance, Apr. 2021; possibly bond counsel, not litigation);
  - $50,000 to Ogletree Deakins for FY2026 human-resources legal services (Dec. 2025) [35].
- **Lawsuits are largely insured.** The FY2025 audited report lists educators' legal liability coverage with a **$10,000 deductible and a $15 million limit**. It states that settled claims have not exceeded coverage in any of the past three years. Management judges that pending claims will not materially affect the district's finances [5].
- **Upper bound.** Legal work is one part of "purchased services." CH-UH's purchased services total about **$3,255 per pupil, roughly the same as similar districts' $3,323** (OpenHeights calculation from the FY2025 District Profile: 11.8% × $27,676 vs. 19.1% × $17,424) [8]. Even if CH-UH's legal bills were well above its peers', they could explain only a small part of its overall spending premium.
- **How to get the real number.** A public-records request for all payments to law firms over several years would give the total. Payment amounts are generally public even when the details of legal work are privileged.
