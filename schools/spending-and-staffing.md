# Spending, Staffing, and Facilities

[← Back to the CH-UH schools guide](./)

*Cleveland Heights-University Heights City School District (CH-UH). Research current as of October 12, 2026. This page covers how the district spends money and how it is staffed. Separate pages cover the Issue 4 levy and five-year forecast, revenue and taxes, enrollment, academics, and EdChoice.*

> **Verification status: preliminary.** This page was compiled from search-indexed excerpts of the cited sources; during research the primary documents (state report card files, district PDFs, county tables) could not be opened directly. Every figure links to its source and gives its year, but has not yet been checked line-by-line against the original document. Items we could not confirm are listed under "Gaps / unverified." See [About this guide](./#about-this-guide).

## Key facts

- **Spending per pupil depends on the measure used.** Federal (NCES) data for FY2023 (school year 2022–23) shows **total current expenditures of about $24,221 per pupil**, of which **instruction was $13,896 per pupil (about 57%)** [1][2]. NCES also lists **total expenditures (including capital and debt) of $138.0 million, or $27,552 per student** [1][3]. Ohio's own "per equivalent pupil" measure is much lower because it weights students with higher needs (see below) [4][5].
- **The district's audited program expenses were about $136.2 million in FY2024, up from about $132.7 million in FY2023** [6][7]. That is an increase of about 2.6% (OpenHeights calculation: 136.2 ÷ 132.7 − 1 ≈ 2.6%).
- **Staffing (NCES, 2024–25):** 5,019 students, **347.54 classroom teacher FTEs**, a **student/teacher ratio of 14.44**, and **1,211.91 total staff** [1][8]. For 2023–24, Ballotpedia, citing NCES, reports 350.75 teachers and a ratio of 14.02 [9].
- **2026 cuts:** On May 6, 2026 the Board approved eliminating **32 positions effective July 1, 2026** (about 3% of the workforce), saving about **$3.4 million** in the general fund. Because of contractual "bumping" rights, **21 staff contracts were suspended** [10][11][12]. The district says it plans **at least $5 million in cuts over two years** [10].
- **Teacher contract:** The district lists a **2025–2028 agreement with the Cleveland Heights Teachers Union (CHTU, AFT Local 795)** and 2025–2028 agreements with OAPSE Locals 102 and 617 [13][14]. We could not find the raise percentages; see Gaps. The previous CHTU contract (2022–2025) was a three-year deal ratified by 92% of members [15].
- **Facilities:** In November 2013, voters passed **Issue 81, a $134.8 million bond**, with about 59% in favor. It funded Phase 1: rebuilding or renovating Heights High and Roxboro and Monticello middle schools. Wiley Middle School closed [16][17]. The district has described the Phase 1 effort as a "$159 million" project, which suggests the bond did not cover the full cost [16]. Phase 2, the elementary buildings, was left for a future community decision [17].

## 1. Total and per-pupil spending

### Why per-pupil figures disagree

| Measure | What it divides | CH-UH value | Year | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NCES total current expenditures per pupil | Day-to-day operating spending ÷ enrolled students | ~$24,221 | FY2023 | [1][2] |
| NCES total expenditures per student (includes capital/debt) | All spending ($138.0M) ÷ students | $27,552 | FY2023 (as listed) | [1][3] |
| NCES F-33 per-pupil (via aggregator) | Current spending ÷ students | $24,456 | FY2022 | [18] |
| U.S. News (NCES-based) | Total expenses ÷ students | $22,447 | year not stated | [19] |
| Niche | Expenses ÷ students | $25,186 | year not stated | [20] |
| Ohio Dept. of Education (as reported ~2020) | Operating spending ÷ *weighted* "equivalent pupils" | $14,404 (vs. district-profile figure of $21,222) | ~FY2019 | [4] |

**What "per equivalent pupil" means.** Ohio's report card ranks districts by *expenditure per equivalent pupil*. This measure divides spending by a weighted enrollment count that gives extra weight to students with disabilities, economically disadvantaged students, and English learners. *Expenditure per pupil* divides by plain average daily membership [5]. A district that enrolls many higher-need students will look cheaper per *equivalent* pupil than per *enrolled* pupil. In about 2020, CH-UH officials attributed the gap between $21,222 and $14,404 to the state's special-education weighting. They said students with disabilities made up about **19%** of enrollment at the time [4]. That share is a district statement and is not independently verified here.

**Ohio benchmark.** NCES reports Ohio's statewide current expenditure per pupil at **$15,684 in FY2023** [21]. Using the NCES figures above, CH-UH spent about **54% more per enrolled pupil than the state average** (OpenHeights calculation: $24,221 ÷ $15,684 ≈ 1.54). This uses an unweighted denominator, so it does not adjust for student need.

**Ten-year trend.** We could not get a consistent ten-year series of CH-UH per-pupil spending from DEW's Expenditure Flow Model or from NCES. This is listed under Gaps.

### Spending on resident students who do not attend district schools

Spending per *enrolled* pupil can overstate costs per student actually served. Ohio districts must transport many resident students who attend private and charter schools. CH-UH also carries costs tied to EdChoice:

- In 2020, a district official noted that public districts "have to transport those kids as well," including voucher users [26].
- About **1,400 resident students used EdChoice vouchers in 2019–20**. The district reported a cost of roughly **$7 million**, and its CFO said 94% of those students had never attended district schools [26][27].
- A 2020 Heights Observer letter disputing an earlier opinion piece put the **2019–20 EdChoice deduction at $7,074,113**, with **$4,286,412 funded by the state**. That leaves a net cost of about **$2.79 million** (OpenHeights calculation: $7,074,113 − $4,286,412 = $2,787,701). These figures are contested [28].

Since FY2022, Ohio pays EdChoice scholarships directly instead of deducting them from districts. Transportation obligations remain. We did not find a dollar figure for CH-UH's cost of transporting nonpublic students, or for state auxiliary services funds passed through for nonpublic schools (see Gaps). EdChoice is covered in detail on its own page.

## 2. Staffing

| Metric | Value | Year | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Students | 5,019 | 2024–25 | [1][8] |
| Classroom teachers (FTE) | 347.54 | 2024–25 | [1][8] |
| Classroom teachers (FTE) | 350.75 | 2023–24 | [9] |
| Student/teacher ratio | 14.44 | 2024–25 | [8] |
| Student/teacher ratio | 14.02 | 2023–24 | [9] |
| Total staff (NCES) | 1,211.91 | 2024–25 | [8] |
| Staff per 100 students | 24.1 | 2024–25 | OpenHeights calculation: 1,211.91 ÷ 5,019 × 100 |
| Teachers per 100 students | 6.9 | 2024–25 | OpenHeights calculation: 347.54 ÷ 5,019 × 100 |
| CHTU bargaining unit size | ~500 (teachers, counselors, nurses, others) | 2020 | [29] |

**Workforce size conflict.** The district said 32 positions were "approximately 3%" of its workforce [10]. That implies a workforce of about **1,070** (OpenHeights calculation: 32 ÷ 0.03 ≈ 1,067). NCES reports 1,211.91 total staff [8]. An older report cited by search results listed 959 staff, but we could not identify its source or year. The figures likely differ in what they count (FTE vs. headcount, substitutes, part-time and contracted staff) and in year.

**Staff vs. enrollment trend.** We could not assemble a reliable ten-year series of staff FTEs to compare against enrollment. A common efficiency question is whether staffing has kept pace with enrollment decline. Data for that test is listed under Gaps. Comparisons should use DEW EMIS staff data, not the mixed aggregator figures.

**Teacher experience.** A 2018 Heights Observer piece by the CHTU president reported years of district service for union members: 204 with fewer than 10 years, 222 with 10–19 years, 90 with 20–29 years, and 8 with 30 or more [30]. That totals 524 (OpenHeights calculation). It is a union-provided snapshot from eight years ago.

**Average teacher salary.** We found no verified current district average. Crowd-sourced sites (Glassdoor, Indeed) are not reliable for this purpose [31]. DEW publishes average teacher salary and experience on district report cards. Those figures were not retrievable for this page.

## 3. Recent cuts (2026)

| Item | Detail | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Board action | May 6, 2026: 32 positions eliminated, effective July 1, 2026 | [10] |
| Positions | 11 classified staff, 7 instructional coaches, 4 Title I teachers (the district cites federal funding changes), 4 administrators, 2 social workers, and others | [10][12] |
| People affected | 21 contracts suspended; others moved into open positions through bumping rights | [10][11] |
| Savings | ~$3.4M in the general fund from reductions in force, plus cuts to department budgets, professional development, and supplemental positions | [10][11] |
| District's stated cause | Incomplete phase-in of the state Fair School Funding Plan. The district says full implementation would have added about $7M over two years | [10] |
| Cash outlook (district) | About −$6M cash balance by 2028–29 even after cuts. Reliance on reserves since 2024–25 | [10][11] |

Per position, the reductions average about **$106,000 in salary and benefits** (OpenHeights calculation: $3.4M ÷ 32 ≈ $106,250). This is a rough average, since some savings may come from non-RIF items.

**Earlier precedent.** In 2016 the district cut more than 50 positions, mostly teachers, to save about $3.5 million per year, citing enrollment decline [35].

**Two readings.** Critics of district spending point to above-state-average spending per enrolled pupil, a low student/teacher ratio (about 14:1), and a large total staff relative to enrollment. The district and union point to cost drivers outside the district's control: a high share of students with disabilities [4], obligations to transport and serve nonpublic students [26], and voucher losses [27]. A Heights Observer column argued the 2026 cuts carry a "heavy cost" for students [36].

| Phase 1 scope | Heights High, Roxboro MS, Monticello MS renovated. Wiley MS closed after 2013–14, with 400+ students moved to Roxboro and Monticello | [16][17][37] |

**Bond ratings.** We found no current Moody's or S&P rating for CH-UH (see Gaps). Ratings would be in official statements on MSRB EMMA.

**Auditor of State.** The FY2024 financial audit (July 1, 2023 to June 30, 2024) was done by Rea & Associates and accepted by the Auditor of State, with an opinion dated December 27, 2024 [6][39]. Separate SERS and STRS census examinations for the same year were released in 2024 [39]. Our searches for this page found no findings for recovery in the FY2024 audit. Separately, in May 2021 the Auditor of State issued a **$34,675 finding for recovery** over district-funded levy polling (see [Governance and Timeline](governance-and-timeline)). We could not review the full report text to confirm the absence of findings. No state performance audit of CH-UH was found. Nearby East Cleveland had one in 2024 [39].
