# CH-UH City Schools: An Evidence Guide for the 2026 Levy

*Cleveland Heights-University Heights City School District (CH-UH). Ohio IRN 043794 · NCES ID 3904379. Compiled October 12, 2026.*

On **November 3, 2026**, voters in the CH-UH district will decide **Issue 4**, a 6.5-mill continuing operating levy. This guide gathers what the public record shows about the district's money, its students and its results. It lays out the evidence and the arguments on both sides. OpenHeights takes no position on the levy.

> **Verification status: preliminary.** The figures in this guide come from search-indexed text of the sources cited on each page: state reports, district documents, federal data and local news. During research, the primary files themselves could not be opened directly. Every number links to its source and gives its year, but has not yet been checked line by line against the original. Several important questions remain open; see [What we don't know yet](#what-we-dont-know-yet). Corrections are welcome.

## What the evidence shows

### 1. The money: why the district keeps going back to voters

The district's finances follow a pattern built into Ohio law, made worse by two local factors.

- **HB 920 freezes voted-levy revenue.** When property is reappraised, Ohio cuts the effective rate on voted levies so each one collects about the same dollars as when it passed. CH-UH has 139.2 voted mills but collects only 71.2 effective mills. The 2024 reappraisal raised Cleveland Heights values about 44%, but that did not raise what the voted levies collect. The district's costs, mostly salaries and benefits, grow every year. A district well above the 20-mill floor, as CH-UH is, has no automatic revenue growth and has to keep asking voters. ([Revenue](revenue-and-taxes))
- **The state pays a small share.** About 70% of CH-UH revenue is local, against about 51% statewide. Ohio's funding formula treats the district as property-wealthy, so its formula state share is about 13%. Rising property values can *lower* state aid further. The district says incomplete phase-in of the Fair School Funding Plan cost it about $7M over two years. ([Revenue](revenue-and-taxes))
- **Enrollment has fallen by a third.** Fixed costs (buildings, transportation, central services) are spread over fewer students. Many resident families choose private schools, often with state vouchers. ([Enrollment](enrollment-and-demographics); [EdChoice](school-choice-edchoice))

**How big is the gap?** *OpenHeights calculation:* the forecast balance falls $35.9M over FY2027–FY2030, about $12M a year. Issue 4's $11.1M a year is about the same size. If the forecast is accurate, the levy roughly closes the projected gap. It does not build a large cushion, and it would not stop the HB 920 squeeze from returning in later years.

**What would failure mean in dollars?** *Illustrative OpenHeights calculation:* the 2026 cuts saved about $106,000 per eliminated position on average ($3.4M ÷ 32). The levy's $11.1M equals roughly 100 such positions. That shows the scale; it is not a district plan. We found no published list of cuts the district would make if Issue 4 fails. ([Levy](levy-2026))

### 2. Spending: high per pupil, but the measures differ

CH-UH spends about **$24,200 per enrolled pupil** (NCES, FY2023), about **54% above the Ohio average**. It has about 14 students per teacher and roughly 24 total staff per 100 students. That is the core of the skeptics' "spending, not funding" argument, and it is real.

Several factors partly explain the figure:

- **Special education.** The district has said about 19% of its students have disabilities, well above typical rates. Ohio's own "per equivalent pupil" measure, which weights higher-need students, put CH-UH at $14,404 against $21,222 unweighted (around FY2019).
- **Shrinking enrollment.** Fixed costs are divided by a smaller base.
- **Services to nonpublic students.** The district must transport private-school students it does not enroll.
- **Peer spending.** Neighboring Shaker Heights spends about the same per student on a comparable basis.

We could not build a ten-year staff-versus-enrollment series. That series would show whether staffing shrank in line with the one-third enrollment drop, and it is the most direct test of the efficiency argument. ([Spending](spending-and-staffing))

### 3. Results: low raw scores, improving growth

- **Raw achievement is low but rising.** The Performance Index rose from 69.2 to 79 in two years, and Achievement moved from 2 to 3 stars. The district has rated "meets standards" overall (3.5 stars) for two straight years, after 2.5 stars in 2023-24. ([Academics](academic-outcomes))
- **Growth measures have improved more than raw scores.** Ohio's value-added Progress rating rose from 2 to 4 stars over the same years, meaning students grew more than predicted from their own past scores. Gap Closing reached 5 stars. Harvard and Stanford's Education Recovery Scorecard named CH-UH among Ohio districts "leading the way" in reading recovery and on its 2024-25 "Districts on the Rise" list. ([Adjusted](adjusted-outcomes))
- **Results compared with similar districts.** Stanford's 2022-25 report places CH-UH around the 11th national percentile in math, above the ~5th-percentile average of districts it classes as demographically similar. The evidence we could verify suggests the district performs **at or somewhat above** what its student population predicts. It does not suggest the district is failing its students relative to similar districts, or that it is an outstanding outlier. ([Adjusted](adjusted-outcomes))
- **The racial gap is large.** In 2021-22, 20% of Black students and 73% of white students were proficient, a gap of about 53 points. The district says the gap is narrowing. The 5-star Gap Closing rating measures whether subgroups hit state targets, not the size of the gap. ([Academics](academic-outcomes))

**Reasons for caution about the trend:**

- Ohio scores rose statewide over the same period.
- CH-UH's pandemic math drop (about 1.6 grade levels) was unusually large, so part of the rebound is likely recovery to its earlier level.
- Most of the 2025-26 figures come from the district's own announcements and have not yet been checked against state data files.

### 4. Vouchers: a large and contested factor

Voucher use in the Heights is heavy. About 1,400 residents used vouchers in 2019-20, and about 94% of them had never attended the district.

The financial effect changed in FY2022. Vouchers had been deducted from the district's state aid (about $7.1M gross in 2019-20; the net cost was disputed at about $2.8M after state credits). Since FY2022 the state pays them directly. Current claims that vouchers "cost" the district describe indirect effects (lost enrollment, transportation, and the political economy of state funding), not a line-item deduction. Pre-2022 figures should not be read as current costs.

CH-UH is a lead plaintiff in the lawsuit that won a June 2025 trial ruling holding EdChoice unconstitutional. The ruling is stayed. The appeals court heard argument in May 2026, and we found no decision as of this writing. ([EdChoice](school-choice-edchoice))

## The strongest case on each side

**For Issue 4**
- The deficit appears in the district's state-required forecast, and Ohio districts cannot legally run a deficit. Without new revenue, the alternative is deeper cuts.
- The district cut $3.4M and 32 positions before going to the ballot, and this is its first new operating request in six years.
- The pressure is largely structural: HB 920 holds voted-levy revenue flat, the state share is low and falling, and the funding plan was not fully phased in.
- Results are improving on the measures that best isolate the school's contribution (value-added, recovery), and a sharp cut could undercut that progress.

**Against Issue 4**
- Heights homeowners already pay some of the highest property taxes in Ohio's highest-taxed county. The city lists a total effective rate of $117.68 per $1,000 of assessed value, about 4.1% of market value a year. This levy adds roughly 9% to the school portion (*OpenHeights calculation:* 6.5 ÷ 71.2).
- Spending per enrolled pupil is about 54% above the state average, with a low student-teacher ratio. Enrollment has fallen a third, and it is not shown that staffing fell with it. Critics argue for an independent performance audit before new taxes.
- A continuing levy never expires, and under HB 920 the district will be back for more.
- The forecast's deficit year differs by source (FY2029 vs. FY2030), and state funding could change. Critics argue the district could cut further or wait.

## What we don't know yet

These are the most decision-relevant gaps. Each could be filled from public data.

1. **The five-year forecast line items and assumptions**: salary growth, state aid, and the "if the levy fails" scenario. *Source: the district treasurer, or the Ohio five-year forecast portal.*
2. **Staffing versus enrollment over ten years**: the key efficiency test. *Source: DEW EMIS staff data, district profile (Cupp) reports.*
3. **A demographically adjusted performance residual**: Performance Index against % economically disadvantaged across all ~600 Ohio districts, plus SEDA learning rates for CH-UH and peers. *Source: DEW report card data download; edopportunity.org.*
4. **Current subgroup results and racial gaps**: 2023-24 through 2025-26. *Source: report card Gap Closing detail.*
5. **Current voucher counts and charter enrollment for CH-UH residents**. *Source: DEW scholarship data.*
6. **Peer-district comparisons** on spending, revenue, millage and outcomes: Shaker Heights, South Euclid-Lyndhurst, Euclid, Lakewood and others.
7. **CH-UH's exact effective millage rank** in Cuyahoga County. *Source: County Fiscal Officer tax rate tables.*

### Questions voters may want to ask the district

- Which specific positions and programs would be cut if Issue 4 fails, and in what order?
- How has total staffing (FTE) changed since 2015, compared with enrollment?
- What salary and benefit growth does the forecast assume, and what does the 2025-2028 teacher contract provide?
- If Issue 4 passes, how many years does the forecast show before another new levy would be needed?
- What would the forecast look like if the state completed the Fair School Funding Plan phase-in?
