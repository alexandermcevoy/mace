# CH-UH City Schools: An Evidence Guide for the 2026 Levy

*Cleveland Heights-University Heights City School District (CH-UH). Ohio IRN 043794 · NCES ID 3904379. Updated October 12, 2026.*

On **November 3, 2026**, voters in the CH-UH district will decide **Issue 4**, a 6.5-mill continuing operating levy. This guide brings together what the public record shows about the district's money, its students and its results. It lays out the evidence and the strongest arguments on each side. OpenHeights takes no position on the levy.

> **Verification status: checked against primary sources on Oct. 12, 2026.** Figures on every page were checked against the original documents: state report-card and school-finance data files, the district's five-year forecasts and audited financial reports, county tax-rate tables, Census and NCES data, and the Stanford and Harvard research reports. Each page lists what could not be confirmed under "Gaps / unverified" and records changes from the first draft in a "Corrections log." Corrections are welcome.

## What the evidence shows

### 1. The money: why the district keeps going back to voters

- **Ohio law freezes voted-levy revenue.** Under HB 920, when property is reappraised, the effective rate on voted levies drops so each levy collects about the same dollars it did when it passed.
  - CH-UH has 149.5 voted operating mills, but homeowners paid an effective **51.05**.
  - The 2024 reappraisal raised the district's taxable value 36% (homes about 41%), but that did not raise what the existing levies collect.
  - The district is far above the 20-mill floor, so it gets no automatic growth. Costs grow every year; the forecast assumes benefits rising about 10% a year. ([Revenue](revenue-and-taxes); [Levy](levy-2026))
- **The state pays little, and less over time.** Ohio's formula treats CH-UH as property-wealthy: value per pupil is about 36% above the state median.
  - Its formula state share fell from 13.1% to the legal minimum of 10% in FY2026. Without that minimum it would have been 4.9%, then 0%.
  - Core foundation aid fell from $10.17M to $8.88M. Net state funding stayed about flat because other aid rose.
  - The 2024 reappraisal, by raising local "capacity," also cut the formula's state share. ([Revenue](revenue-and-taxes))
- **Enrollment has fallen by a third since 2000,** and only 55% of the resident students the state counts now attend CH-UH. Fixed costs are spread over fewer students. ([Enrollment](enrollment-and-demographics))

**What Issue 4 does and doesn't fix.** In the district's August 2026 forecast, the balance falls by $7.4M, $11.7M and then $16.8M a year through FY2030.

- **The levy covers the gap for a few years.** An OpenHeights illustration adding the levy's $11.1M a year keeps the balance positive through FY2030 ($35.1M at June 2030).
- **It does not stop the slide.** By FY2030 the yearly shortfall is larger than the levy, so the district would be spending about $5.7M a year more than it takes in. Under HB 920, the pressure that produced this levy will produce another.
- **Earlier forecasts were bleaker.** The February 2026 forecast, made before the cuts, showed the General Fund at −$13.6M in FY2029. That is the source of the superintendent's "run out of cash in 2028–29." ([Levy](levy-2026))

**What would failure mean in dollars?** *Illustrative OpenHeights calculation:* the 2026 cuts saved about $106,000 per eliminated position ($3.4M ÷ 32). The levy's $11.1M equals roughly 100 such positions. That shows the scale only; we found no district list of cuts if Issue 4 fails.

### 2. Spending: high on every measure

The first draft of this guide suggested the high per-pupil figure was largely a quirk of measurement. The verified data do not support that reading. **CH-UH spends more than the state average on every measure.**

- **Per enrolled pupil:** about **45% above** the state ($25,025 vs. $17,257, FY2024).
- **Per need-weighted ("equivalent") pupil:** this measure adjusts for disability, poverty and English learners. CH-UH ranks **2nd-highest of 96** similar-size districts, behind only Shaker Heights.
- **Classroom share:** **63.1%**, which ranks 92nd of 96.

**Cost drivers that are real:**
- **Special education:** students with disabilities are 22.2% of enrollment, a share that is rising.
- **Busing:** 67% of the students CH-UH buses attend nonpublic schools.
- **Fixed costs:** a shrinking enrollment spreads them over fewer students.
- **Slower growth:** CH-UH's per-pupil spending grew more slowly than the state's over the last decade (+41% vs. +52%).

**The staffing test, which the skeptics' case rests on, gives mixed results.**
- **Teachers have been cut faster than enrollment:** NCES counts down 21% vs. enrollment down 6% since 2015-16.
- **Total staff grew:** the district's own count of full-time employees rose 7% from FY2016 to FY2025 while enrollment fell 11%. Pupil-support staff rose 71% and administration 19%.
- **Pay is above average:** teachers earn above the state average ($87,540 vs. $76,482) with 16 years' average experience.

Supporters can point to a leaner teaching force and higher student needs. Critics can point to growth in non-teaching staff and a classroom share near the bottom of the district's comparison group. ([Spending](spending-and-staffing))

### 3. Results: improving, about as expected for its students, with real weak spots

- **Raw achievement is below the state but rising fast.**
  - The Performance Index rose from 69.2 to 79.0 in two years, against +1.8 points statewide. It is still below Ohio's 82.8.
  - The district rated 3.5 stars ("meets standards") in 2024-25 and 2025-26. It was 3 stars in 2022-23 and dipped to 2.5 in 2023-24.
  - Earlier history: overall "D" in 2017-18 and 2018-19; "Continuous Improvement" 2006-07 to 2011-12. ([Academics](academic-outcomes))
- **Compared with Ohio districts with similar child poverty, CH-UH performs about as predicted.**
  - OpenHeights regressed Performance Index on poverty across all 607 districts. CH-UH's 2025-26 score was 1.1 points above prediction (55th percentile), up from 7.1 points below in 2023-24.
  - With race added as a control it ranks near the top, so the result depends on the model.
  - Ohio's "economically disadvantaged" flag is nearly 100% for CH-UH because of universal free meals, so census child poverty is the better control. ([Adjusted](adjusted-outcomes))
- **The growth measures disagree.**
  - Ohio's value-added rose from 2 to 4 stars; the 2025-26 growth index ranks at the 87th percentile.
  - Stanford's 2022–25 learning rate for grades 3–8 is **0.76 grade levels a year**. That is the 14th percentile nationally, below similar districts (0.92) and the lowest of 12 Cuyahoga districts compared.
  - Stanford ranks CH-UH's 2022–25 *trend* at the 96th percentile in math, mostly recovery from an unusually large pandemic drop.
  - Read together: recent improvement is real, but students still gain less per year than in comparable districts. ([Adjusted](adjusted-outcomes))
- **Gaps are large, but both groups outperform their statewide peers.**
  - In 2025-26, 47.8% of Black students and 91.6% of white students were proficient in English language arts. Statewide the figures were 39.2% and 69.7%.
  - Both groups do better than their statewide counterparts. The 44-point gap is wider than the state's 30 points mainly because CH-UH's white students score so high.
  - The gap has narrowed since 2023-24. ([Academics](academic-outcomes))
- **Weak spots:**
  - **Chronic absenteeism** is 37.9%, against 23.9% statewide.
  - **Grade 8 math** proficiency is 32.3% vs. 52.0% statewide.
  - **Grade 4** scores and growth fell in 2025-26.

### 4. Vouchers and the families who leave

- **CH-UH is Ohio's most voucher-heavy district.** By state payment data it had the highest EdChoice share of resident students of any of Ohio's 609 districts in FY2019–FY2021, reaching 24.4% in FY2021. Resident scholarship users rose from 1,295 to 2,938 between 2018-19 and 2025-26; universal eligibility since 2023 drove most of the recent growth.
- **About a third of resident children are in private school or homeschool.** That share (34%) has been stable since about 2010 and is well above peers: Shaker Heights 22%, South Euclid-Lyndhurst 25%, Euclid 28%, Lakewood 17%.
- **Most voucher users never attended the district.** About 94% of 2019-20 voucher users had never enrolled in CH-UH. ([EdChoice](school-choice-edchoice); [Enrollment](enrollment-and-demographics))
- **The money changed in FY2022.** Until FY2021, vouchers were deducted from district aid ($7.08M in FY2020, $8.94M in FY2021). Since FY2022 the state pays them directly. Claims that vouchers "cost" the district a specific sum today describe indirect effects, not a budget line.
- **The lawsuit is pending.** CH-UH is a plaintiff in *Columbus City School District et al. v. State of Ohio*. EdChoice was ruled unconstitutional at trial in June 2025; the ruling is stayed. The appeal was argued May 12, 2026, and no decision had been issued as of this writing. ([EdChoice](school-choice-edchoice))
- **Students and community look different.** District students are 69% Black and 17% white, while the district's school-age children are 49% Black and 40% white. That reflects who opts out. ([Enrollment](enrollment-and-demographics))

## The strongest case on each side

**For Issue 4**
- **The deficit is documented and law-driven.** The state-required forecast shows the General Fund going negative by FY2030 even after $3.4M in cuts, and Ohio districts cannot run deficits. HB 920 means existing levies don't grow, and the state has cut its formula share to the legal minimum. Without new local revenue, the only option is deeper cuts.
- **The ask is infrequent.** It is the first new operating levy since 2020, and CH-UH accepts no open-enrollment students, so its budget serves residents only.
- **Results are improving.** Achievement and value-added are up, with the highest Performance Index in six years. Results now match what poverty predicts, and Black and white students both outperform their statewide peers. Deep cuts risk reversing that.
- **Many costs are obligations.** Much of the cost structure is mandated or need-driven: special education (22% of students) and busing nonpublic students (two-thirds of riders).

**Against Issue 4**
- **Taxes are already among the highest in Ohio.** The school operating rate on homes is 4th-highest of 606 districts, and total property tax runs about 2.9% of market value a year. Issue 4 raises effective school operating millage on homes by about **12.7%** (6.5 ÷ 51.05) and the total residential tax rate by about **7%** (6.5 ÷ 92.30). *(OpenHeights calculations.)*
- **Spending is high on every measure, and staff grew while enrollment fell.** Spending is about 45% above the state per pupil and 2nd of 96 per need-weighted pupil, and the classroom share ranks 92nd of 96. Full-time staff rose 7% while enrollment fell 11%, mostly in non-teaching roles.
- **It buys time, not stability.** By the district's own forecast the yearly shortfall exceeds the levy by FY2030. Without spending changes, voters can expect another request; and as a continuing levy, Issue 4 never expires.
- **The outcomes aren't a strong case.** Results are about average for the district's poverty level despite top-tier spending. Stanford's learning rate trails similar districts, and chronic absenteeism is very high.

## What we don't know yet

1. **The full August 2026 forecast lines** (revenue and spending totals), and any district list of cuts if Issue 4 fails. *Ask the treasurer.*
2. **Scholarship dollars paid for CH-UH residents since FY2022.** Headcounts are known; per-district dollar amounts are not published.
3. **Why "economically disadvantaged" sits near 100%,** and the exact effect on Ohio's measures.
4. **The district's bond ratings** and the 2022–25 contract raise terms.
5. **The outcome of the EdChoice appeal.**

### Questions voters may want to ask the district

- Which positions and programs would be cut if Issue 4 fails, and in what order?
- Why has pupil-support and administrative staffing grown since FY2016 while enrollment fell, and what has it bought?
- The forecast shows the yearly shortfall exceeding the levy by FY2030. What is the plan for that, and when would the next levy be needed?
- What would the forecast look like if the state restored the funding formula's phase-in?
- What is the plan for chronic absenteeism (38%) and middle-school math?
