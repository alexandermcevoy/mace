# Outcomes Adjusted for Who the District Serves

[← Back to the CH-UH schools guide](./)

*Research status as of October 12, 2026. This page answers a narrower question than a raw report card: **given the students Cleveland Heights-University Heights City Schools (CH-UH) serves, how much are they learning, and is that improving?** Several parts of this question could not be fully verified during research. Those parts are marked plainly and listed under [Gaps / unverified](#gaps--unverified). Everything else carries a source link and a data year.*

> **Verification status: preliminary.** This page was compiled from search-indexed excerpts of the cited sources; during research the primary documents (state report card files, district PDFs, county tables) could not be opened directly. Every figure links to its source and gives its year, but has not yet been checked line-by-line against the original document. Items we could not confirm are listed under "Gaps / unverified." See [About this guide](./#about-this-guide). Figures read from search-indexed text of a PDF are marked "(search-indexed text)".

## Key facts / bottom line

- **Achievement levels are low, which mostly reflects who the district serves.** In the Stanford Educational Opportunity Project's 2025 trends report (school years 2022–25), CH-UH's average math performance ranked **9,056th of 10,205 U.S. districts**, higher than about **11%** of districts. Its reading ranked **7,822nd of 10,076**, about the **22nd percentile** [1] (search-indexed text). Levels like these track family income and background very closely nationwide.
- **CH-UH ranks above the "similar districts" Stanford compares it to.** The same report puts the similar-district average at about **9,677th of 10,205** in math, roughly the 5th percentile [1] (search-indexed text). If that is right, CH-UH scores above districts with comparable demographics. Comparison with similar districts is the closest thing to an adjusted measure this research could verify.
- **Pandemic loss was large in math, and recovery since 2022 has been strong by national standards.** According to the Education Recovery Scorecard's January 2024 district report [2] (search-indexed text), CH-UH's grade 3–8 math average went from **−1.03** grade levels below the 2019 national average (2019) to **−2.62** (2022) to **−1.92** (2023). Reading went from **−0.57** to **−1.15** to **−0.95** over the same years.
- **Outside researchers have singled out the recent gains.** The Education Recovery Scorecard's 2025 Ohio page lists CH-UH with Euclid City and Fairborn City among Ohio districts **"leading the way" in reading** [3]. Its "Districts on the Rise" list for 2024–25 includes CH-UH for **math and reading** [4].
- **Ohio's value-added (Progress) rating has risen.** District Progress went from **2 stars (2023–24)** to **3 stars (2024–25)**, which means students met expected growth [5][6]. The district says Progress rose again to **4 stars in 2025–26**, with Heights High rated **5 stars** on Progress [7]. Because Progress compares each student's growth with what was expected from their own prior scores, it is the state's main measure that partly adjusts for background.
- **Honest assessment.** The verified evidence fits this picture: a district with low raw achievement that is roughly in line with or somewhat above what its demographics predict, and that has improved since 2022 on both national (Stanford/Harvard) and state (value-added) growth measures. **Not verified here:** a regression-based "residual" against poverty, CH-UH's rank within its Ohio typology, subgroup-versus-state comparisons, and any cost-effectiveness figure. Treat this page as partial evidence, not a final verdict.

## Why adjustment matters

Raw test scores mostly measure who attends a school. Nationally, Stanford's SEDA research finds that a district's socioeconomic makeup explains a large share of the variation in average test scores between districts. Two districts with equally effective teaching can therefore post very different raw scores. Adjusted measures try to separate the school's contribution from the starting point students bring:

| Measure | What it captures | How much it depends on family background |
|---|---|---|
| Average score level (Ohio Achievement/Performance Index; SEDA "average test scores") | Where students are | High |
| Change over time / recovery (Education Recovery Scorecard) | Whether a district is improving relative to its own past | Lower, but can reflect changes in who enrolls |
| Learning rate (SEDA grade 3→8 slope) | How much students gain per school year | Lower; Stanford presents it as closer to school contribution |
| Value-added (Ohio Progress, EVAAS) | Student growth compared with growth predicted from each student's own prior scores | Lowest of these, but still imperfect |
| Comparison with "similar districts" or regression residuals | Performance compared with districts that have similar demographics | Depends on which variables are controlled |

No single measure is clean. That is why this page puts several side by side.

## 1. Stanford Educational Opportunity Project (SEDA) and Education Recovery Scorecard

**District identifier.** The Stanford/Harvard reports file CH-UH under NCES LEA ID **3904379** (see the URLs for [1] and [2]); its Ohio IRN is 043794.

### 1a. Pandemic loss and recovery (Education Recovery Scorecard, 2024 release)

Grade 3–8 averages, in grade-level equivalents relative to the **2019 national average** (0 = national average in 2019) [2] (search-indexed text):

| Subject | 2019 | 2022 | 2023 | Change 2019→22 | Change 2022→23 | Net change 2019→23 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Math | −1.03 | −2.62 | −1.92 | −1.59 | +0.70 | −0.89 |
| Reading | −0.57 | −1.15 | −0.95 | −0.58 | +0.20 | −0.38 |

*The change columns are an OpenHeights calculation (later year minus earlier year). For example, math 2019→22 is −2.62 − (−1.03) = −1.59, which matches the −1.59 change that appears in the report's indexed text [2]. Reading 2019→22 is −1.15 − (−0.57) = −0.58. Net math 2019→23 is −1.92 − (−1.03) = −0.89.*

How to read this: in 2019, CH-UH students in grades 3–8 were on average about one grade level behind the national average in math and about half a grade behind in reading. By 2022, math had fallen by roughly 1.6 grade levels. That is a very large drop by national standards. CH-UH then regained a substantial share in a single year.

**Contradiction found.** One search-indexed summary of the same PDF gave 2023 values of **−2.48 (math)** and **−1.80 (reading)**. These do not match the −1.92 and −0.95 that appeared consistently in two other readings. The two sets may come from different subgroups or different tables in the PDF. This must be checked against the PDF itself before publication.

### 1b. 2025 trends report (SEDA, school years 2022–25)

Stanford's 2025 "Trends in Academic Performance" report for CH-UH [1] (search-indexed text) gives these national rankings:

| | CH-UH rank | Approx. percentile | "Similar districts" average |
|---|---|---|---|
| Math, 2022–25 | 9,056 of 10,205 | ~11th | ~9,677 of 10,205 (~5th pct.) |
| Reading, 2022–25 | 7,822 of 10,076 | ~22nd | not verified |

*OpenHeights calculation of percentile: (10,205 − 9,056) / 10,205 ≈ 11.3%. The report itself describes the math figure as "higher than 11% of districts."*

The same report gives grade-level averages and annual trend rates since 2022. These did not come through clearly enough to publish: the indexed text gave different values for math and reading, including "−1.66 grade levels" and trends of "−0.04" and "+0.05 grade levels/year". The report also has subgroup rankings by race and income. One reading suggested White students in CH-UH rank near the top nationally while Black and low-income students rank near the middle, but those figures were too garbled to use. See [Gaps](#gaps--unverified).

### 1c. Recognition in the 2025 Education Recovery Scorecard

- The Ohio state page of the 2025 Education Scorecard lists CH-UH, with **Euclid City** and **Fairborn City**, among the districts "leading the way" in **reading** [3].
- The Scorecard's **"Districts on the Rise" (2024–25, math and reading)** list includes CH-UH [4]. Other Ohio districts on that list include Euclid, Fairborn and Marion City (search-indexed text).
- For context, the same Ohio page says Ohio as a whole ranked **14th of 38 states in math growth** and **24th of 35 in reading growth** from 2022 to 2025. It also says Ohio's achievement remained about one-third of a grade level below 2019 in math and over half a grade level below in reading [3].

## 2. Ohio's Progress component (value-added)

Ohio's Progress component uses EVAAS value-added. Each student's growth is compared with growth predicted from that student's own prior test history, and the results are combined over up to three years. Three stars means students on average met expected growth. More stars means they grew more than expected; fewer means less.

| Report card year | District Progress | Overall rating | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2023–24 | 2 stars (implied: 2024–25 was "one star higher") | 2.5 stars | [5][6] |
| 2024–25 | 3 stars | 3.5 stars | [5][6] |
| 2025–26 | 4 stars (district: "increased one star") | 3.5 stars | [7] |
| 2022–23 and earlier | Not verified | Not verified | — |

School-level Progress, 2025–26, as reported by the district [7]: **Heights High 5 stars**. **Roxboro Middle and one other school 4 stars.** The indexed text names Baumgardner, which may be a misreading; verify. **Boulevard, Fairfax and Gearity elementary schools 3 stars.** For 2024–25, the district said **seven of 10 schools** met or exceeded expected growth [5].

**Subgroup value-added** (for example, Progress for economically disadvantaged students, students with disabilities, or the lowest 20%) is published on the state report card but **was not retrieved**.

Other 2024–25 and 2025–26 ratings reported by the district and local press: Achievement **2 stars** in 2024–25 [6]. A Performance Index of **74.6 in 2024–25, up from 69.2** in 2023–24, which the district calls its highest in five years [5] (district-reported, search-indexed text; verify on reportcard.education.ohio.gov). Gap Closing **5 stars in 2025–26**, up one star [7]. Weighted graduation rate **93.0%** (2025–26 release) [7]. The district and News 5 said 2024–25 was the first time in more than a decade that the district was rated as meeting state standards overall [5][6].

## 4. Performance relative to poverty (residual analysis)

**Not computed.** The ideal test is CH-UH's residual from a regression of Ohio Performance Index on % economically disadvantaged. That requires the full statewide file of about 600 districts' PI and econ-disadvantaged share. The file could not be downloaded during this research. Under this project's evidence rules, no residual is estimated and no peer figures are quoted from memory.

What can be said from verified sources:

- Stanford's similar-district comparison [1] suggests CH-UH's math level is **above** the average of districts with comparable demographics (about the 11th versus about the 5th percentile nationally). This is a published, demographically matched comparison, though the matching criteria were not verified.
- The 2025 Scorecard groups CH-UH with **Euclid** (a nearby, higher-poverty inner-ring district) as a reading recovery leader [3][4]. Recovery is measured against each district's own past, so this comparison is less sensitive to demographics.

**Method to use** when the data is available: for every Ohio district, regress PI on % economically disadvantaged (optionally adding % students with disabilities and % English learners). Report CH-UH's actual PI, predicted PI and residual. Name the year, and note that the residual is a descriptive gap, not a causal effect.

## 5. Cost-effectiveness

**No outcome-per-dollar figure is published here.** Spending per pupil is covered on the finance page of this site. Linking spending to the outcomes above needs care, for three reasons:

1. **The nonpublic-student distortion.** Ohio's "expenditure per pupil" divides some costs by public enrollment, but CH-UH spends money on students who don't attend its schools. EdChoice voucher deductions, transportation for private-school students and auxiliary services all flow through the district's books. Districts with high private-school or voucher participation, which CH-UH has (see the demographics and finance pages), can therefore look more expensive per pupil than they are.
2. **Need-based costs.** Higher-poverty districts and districts with more students with disabilities face higher costs to reach the same outcomes. Comparing raw dollars against raw achievement penalizes them.
3. **Levels versus growth.** Comparing spending with Achievement or PI mostly measures demographics. Comparing spending with value-added (Progress) or SEDA learning rates is more meaningful, but no published Ohio analysis doing this for CH-UH was found.

The Thomas B. Fordham Institute's Ohio work and Ohio Excels discuss district efficiency in general. No CH-UH-specific "bang for the buck" figure from them was verified.

## 6. Subgroups: comparing like with like

The cleanest comparison is how CH-UH's **Black** and **economically disadvantaged** students perform against the same groups statewide and in peer districts. This sidesteps differences in district-wide composition. The data exists on the Ohio report card (Gap Closing detail and subgroup Performance Index) and in SEDA's subgroup estimates. **It was not retrieved.**

The Gap Closing component, which compares each subgroup with state targets, rose to **5 stars in 2025–26** [7]. That indicates subgroups met or beat Ohio's targets in that year, but it is not a direct comparison with statewide subgroup averages. The SEDA 2025 report contains race and income subgroup rankings [1], but they could not be read reliably (see Gaps).

## Limitations

- **Composition effects.** District averages move when the mix of students changes. If more higher- or lower-scoring families enter or leave, averages shift even if teaching does not change.
- **Selection through private schools and EdChoice.** Many resident children in Cleveland Heights and University Heights attend private, charter or voucher-funded schools. Who leaves, and whether they would have scored higher or lower, affects CH-UH's averages and its per-pupil spending figures. That is not measured here.
- **Regression to the mean.** Districts with unusually large 2019→22 drops, such as CH-UH's −1.59 grade levels in math, tend to rebound partly by statistical reversion. Some of the 2022→25 "rise" may be bounce-back rather than improvement.
- **Changes to tests and the report card.** Ohio moved from letter grades to a star system in 2021–22 and has adjusted component weights since. SEDA and the Scorecard link state tests to NAEP to put them on a common scale, which adds estimation error, especially for districts of about 5,000 students.
- **Value-added caveats.** EVAAS adjusts for students' prior scores but not directly for poverty. Multi-year composites lag. Star cut points are policy choices.
- **Self-reported figures.** Several numbers (Performance Index 74.6/69.2, school-level Progress, 93.0% graduation rate) come from district press releases [5][7]. They should be checked against the state's data files.
